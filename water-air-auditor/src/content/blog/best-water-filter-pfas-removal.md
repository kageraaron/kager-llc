Not all water filters remove PFAS. This is one of the most important distinctions in home water filtration — because the most popular and affordable options (standard pitcher carbon filters) are largely ineffective against PFAS, while reverse osmosis systems remove 95%+ of them. Here's what the independent testing and NSF certifications actually show.

## Quick answers

**What is the best water filter for PFAS?** Reverse osmosis (RO), certified to NSF/ANSI 58, consistently removes 95%+ of PFAS in independent laboratory testing. The AquaTru countertop RO is the top pick for most households — no installation required.

**Does Brita remove PFAS?** No. Standard Brita pitchers and most basic carbon filters are not certified for PFAS removal and should not be relied upon. The Brita Longlast+ is certified for some contaminants but not PFAS at relevant concentrations.

**Does Berkey remove PFAS?** Partially. Black Berkey elements reduce PFAS, but the manufacturer's claims have faced scrutiny and the reduction is not as complete as RO. Berkey is not certified to NSF 58 for PFAS.

**What NSF certification do I need for PFAS?** NSF/ANSI 58 (reverse osmosis systems) or NSF P473 (activated carbon systems specifically tested for PFAS). Without one of these certifications, you cannot rely on the filter for PFAS reduction.

## Why PFAS is harder to filter than most contaminants

PFAS molecules are small (molecular weights of 200–600 daltons) and highly water-soluble. Unlike many contaminants that can be captured by straightforward activated carbon adsorption, PFAS requires either:

1. **Physical separation** — reverse osmosis membranes block PFAS because the membrane pore size (0.0001 microns) is smaller than PFAS molecules
2. **Targeted activated carbon** — specific carbon media with high surface area and charge characteristics that attract PFAS molecules. Not all carbon performs equally; the quality, density, and residence time of the carbon bed all matter.

This is why cheap carbon pitcher filters underperform. The carbon contact time is too short and the carbon density too low to capture PFAS at concentrations near the new EPA limit of 4 ppt.

## Reverse osmosis: the gold standard

Reverse osmosis forces water under pressure through a semi-permeable membrane with pores small enough to block PFAS, lead, arsenic, nitrates, and many other contaminants. Independent EWG laboratory testing ([EWG PFAS filter study](https://www.ewg.org/research/pfas-filter-study)) found that RO achieved greater than 95% PFAS reduction consistently across brands and PFAS compounds.

**Trade-offs of RO:**
- **Water waste:** Traditional RO wastes 3–4 gallons of water for every gallon produced. Newer systems like AquaTru have improved this ratio substantially.

- **Mineral removal:** RO removes beneficial minerals along with contaminants. If you rely on water for mineral intake, consider remineralization filters or dietary supplementation.
- **Maintenance:** Filters and membrane need replacement every 6–24 months depending on the stage.

## Activated carbon: effective when certified correctly

Activated carbon adsorbs PFAS by exploiting the hydrophobic character of PFAS molecules — they preferentially bind to the carbon surface rather than remaining in water. The critical variable is whether the specific carbon system has been tested and certified for PFAS reduction under realistic conditions.

**NSF P473:** The protocol for testing carbon-based filters for PFOA and PFOS reduction. Filters certified to NSF P473 have been independently verified to reduce these compounds at relevant concentrations.

**What's certified:**
- Clearly Filtered Pitcher (certified to NSF 42/53/401/P473) — the pitcher filter with the strongest PFAS credentials. ~$90
- Epic Pure and Epic Nano pitchers — independently tested for PFAS, though not NSF P473 certified in all configurations
- Some Aquasana under-sink systems with activated carbon specifically rated for PFAS

**What's not reliably effective:**
- Standard Brita (non-Longlast) pitchers
- PUR basic pitchers
- Generic fridge filters
- Most whole-house carbon sediment filters

The EWG study found that even among certified carbon filters, performance varied widely by brand, with some showing inconsistent PFAS reduction at the low concentrations that now matter under the 4 ppt EPA standard.

## Berkey: gravity filter with caveats

The Berkey is popular in prepper and self-sufficiency communities for its gravity-fed design, long element life, and no-electricity operation. The manufacturer claims Black Berkey elements remove PFAS, and some independent testing shows significant reduction. However:

- Berkey is **not certified to NSF 58 or NSF P473** for PFAS specifically
- The company has faced regulatory challenges in some states regarding unverified claims
- Independent tests have produced mixed results — some show good PFAS reduction, others are less consistent

**The verdict on Berkey for PFAS:** It may reduce PFAS meaningfully, but without NSF P473 certification, you cannot be certain of the reduction level at your specific PFAS concentration. If your primary concern is PFAS (rather than general water quality), an NSF-certified RO or carbon system is a more reliable choice.

The Berkey with PF-2 add-on filters does excel for lead reduction and microorganism removal, and is a good option for general water quality improvement in off-grid or emergency preparedness contexts.

## Whole-house vs point-of-use: which do you need?

**Point-of-use (POU):** Filters water at a single tap — typically the kitchen. This is where you drink and cook. POU systems (under-sink RO, countertop RO, certified pitchers) are the most cost-effective way to reduce PFAS in your primary water source.

**Whole-house:** Treats all water entering the home. Required if your concern extends to shower exposure (PFAS can be inhaled through steam), or if you want to filter at every tap. Whole-house activated carbon systems rated for PFAS exist but cost $1,000–$3,000 installed and require professional installation.

For most households with PFAS concerns, **a point-of-use RO for drinking and cooking water** is the highest-value first step. If you want whole-house coverage, add a whole-house carbon system upstream.
