PFAS, Lead & PM2.5 — The Three Pollutants We Track

There are dozens of regulated contaminants. PFAS, lead, and PM2.5 combine ubiquity, severity, and accessible mitigation — meaning if you only fix one thing, fix one of these.

💧

PFAS

Drinking Water

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances — "forever chemicals" used in nonstick coatings, firefighting foam, and stain repellents. They don't break down in nature or in your body.

Health Impacts at a Glance

What the epidemiological literature actually says. Linked sources are peer-reviewed or from public health agencies.

💧

PFAS — What They Do

  • Cancer: EPA classifies PFOA as "likely carcinogenic" and PFOS as having "suggestive evidence." Kidney and testicular cancers most strongly linked.
  • Immune suppression: Reduces vaccine antibody response in children — well-documented across multiple cohorts.
  • Liver effects: Elevated liver enzymes; associations with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.
  • Cholesterol: Higher LDL and total cholesterol with increasing serum PFAS.
  • Developmental: Lower birth weight, accelerated puberty, thyroid disruption.

PFAS bioaccumulate. Half-life in human serum is years to decades — exposure today is exposure for life.

🔩

Lead — What It Does

  • Children: Even very low blood-lead levels are associated with measurable IQ deficits, attention problems, and behavioral changes.
  • Cardiovascular: Lead exposure is now estimated to cause more deaths from cardiovascular disease than from acute poisoning.
  • Kidney: Chronic exposure damages renal function over decades.
  • Pregnancy: Crosses placenta; associated with preterm birth and reduced fetal growth.
  • Neurological (adult): Cognitive decline associated with long-term cumulative exposure.

Lead damage is largely permanent. Prevention is the only effective intervention.

🌫

PM2.5 — What It Does

  • Cardiovascular: Strongest evidence — long-term PM2.5 exposure raises mortality from heart attacks, strokes, and arrhythmias.
  • Respiratory: Worsens asthma, COPD, and increases risk of lung cancer (IARC Group 1 carcinogen).
  • Neurological: Growing evidence linking chronic PM2.5 to dementia and accelerated cognitive aging.
  • Metabolic: Associated with type 2 diabetes incidence and metabolic syndrome.
  • Mortality: Each ~10 µg/m³ increase in long-term exposure tied to ~6–8% increase in all-cause mortality.

No safe threshold has been established. WHO's guideline (5 µg/m³) is well below the U.S. standard.

Highest-Risk Groups

Children, pregnant people, older adults, and anyone with cardiovascular or respiratory disease should weight these exposures most heavily. If you fall into one of these groups, the case for filtration and indoor air management is strong even at "moderate" levels.

If lead is your concern

Any NSF 53-certified carbon block at the kitchen tap will do the job. Look for "lead reduction" specifically — not all carbon filters are certified for it. Replace cartridges on schedule.

If PFAS is your concern

Reverse osmosis at the point of use (under-sink) is the gold standard, removing 95%+ of PFAS. Berkey-style gravity systems with PF-2 cartridges are a secondary option. Standard pitcher carbon is not enough.

If PM2.5 is your concern

True HEPA air purifier sized for the room (target 4–5 air changes/hour). For wildfire smoke or persistent outdoor PM2.5, run continuously and seal the room. MERV 13+ HVAC filters help system-wide.

If you want one solution

For homes with multiple concerns, a whole-house carbon system + under-sink RO + bedroom HEPA covers the realistic threat surface. Total cost: roughly $700–$1,400 for capable mid-tier gear.

Frequently Asked Questions

Science-backed answers to the most common questions about tap water safety, PFAS, lead, PM2.5, and filtration.

Is tap water safe to drink in the United States?

For most people, municipal tap water meets federal legal standards — but "legal" and "safe" are not the same. The EPA's 2024 PFAS limit of 4 parts per trillion reflects decades of evidence, yet an estimated 45% of U.S. tap supplies have detectable PFAS at some level. Roughly 9.2 million homes still connect via lead service lines. Enter your ZIP code above to see what's documented in your area, and check your utility's annual Consumer Confidence Report.

What are PFAS, and are they in my drinking water?

PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are a family of thousands of synthetic chemicals used in nonstick cookware coatings, firefighting foam, water-resistant textiles, and food packaging. They are called "forever chemicals" because they do not break down in the environment or in the human body, accumulating in serum with half-lives of years to decades. The EPA set its first enforceable drinking water limits in April 2024: 4 ppt for PFOA and PFOS. Studies estimate over 200 million Americans are exposed at some level. EPA PFAS standards →

What water filter removes PFAS?

Reverse osmosis (RO) is the most effective point-of-use technology, removing 95%+ of PFAS and certified to NSF/ANSI 58. Two-stage activated carbon block filters certified to NSF P473 can also significantly reduce PFAS, though performance varies by brand. Standard pitcher carbon filters (basic Brita, PUR, etc.) are not certified for PFAS removal and should not be relied upon. For whole-house coverage, a dedicated activated-carbon system rated for PFAS is needed. EWG laboratory testing found reverse osmosis and two-stage carbon achieved >95% PFAS reduction. See the full filtration guide →

Does a Brita or standard pitcher filter remove PFAS or lead?

Standard pitcher filters (basic Brita, PUR, etc.) reduce chlorine and improve taste, but most are not certified to remove PFAS. Some premium pitchers (Clearly Filtered, ZeroWater) are independently tested for PFAS reduction. For lead, look specifically for NSF/ANSI 53 certification labeled "lead reduction" — not all carbon cartridges carry this. An upgrade to an under-sink reverse osmosis system remains the most reliable solution for both PFAS and lead in a single unit.

How do I know if I have lead pipes?

The age of your home is the strongest signal: homes built before 1986 are at highest risk since lead solder and lead service lines were common until the EPA banned them that year. Homes built before 1930 may have all-lead service lines. Under the EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Revision (LCRI), utilities must now provide a service line inventory — contact your utility or check their LCRI disclosure. An at-home water test (like Tap Score) is the only way to confirm actual lead levels at your tap.

What is the EPA's PFAS limit for drinking water?

In April 2024 the EPA finalized the first national drinking water standards for PFAS: 4 parts per trillion (ppt) for PFOA and PFOS individually, and a combined Hazard Index limit for PFNA, PFHxS, HFPO-DA (GenX), and PFBS. Water utilities must achieve compliance by 2029. This is the strictest PFAS drinking water standard in the world. Previously there was no federal enforceable limit. Full EPA rule →

What are the health effects of PFAS exposure?

PFAS accumulate in human tissue with serum half-lives of years to decades. Documented effects include:

  • Immune suppression — reduced vaccine antibody response in children (Grandjean et al., 2012)
  • Cancer — EPA classifies PFOA as "likely carcinogenic"; kidney and testicular cancers most strongly linked
  • Cholesterol — higher LDL and total cholesterol with increasing serum PFAS
  • Developmental — lower birth weight, accelerated puberty, thyroid disruption
  • Liver — elevated enzymes; associations with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

Because exposure accumulates over a lifetime, reducing ongoing intake is the most effective intervention even after past exposure has occurred.

What is PM2.5 and where does it come from?

PM2.5 refers to airborne particulate matter smaller than 2.5 micrometers in diameter — small enough to bypass the nose and throat, penetrate deep into the lungs, and cross into the bloodstream. Sources include vehicle exhaust, wildfire smoke, industrial emissions, wood and biomass burning, and indoor cooking. Because particles are invisible to the naked eye, most people have no idea they're being exposed. Indoor PM2.5 closely tracks outdoor levels unless active HEPA filtration is running. The WHO 2021 guidelines set an annual average target of 5 µg/m³; the U.S. EPA NAAQS is 9 µg/m³.

Does a HEPA air purifier actually reduce PM2.5?

Yes — true HEPA filters capture ≥99.97% of particles ≥0.3 µm, which covers the entire PM2.5 size range. For effective reduction: the filter must be certified "true HEPA" (not "HEPA-type" or "HEPA-like"), the unit must be sized for your room (target 4–5 air changes per hour), and it must run continuously. AHAM Verifide CADR ratings are the most reliable independent measure of real-world performance. During wildfire events, keep windows closed and run the purifier on high.

What is a safe level of PM2.5?

No safe threshold has been established — health effects appear even at very low concentrations. The WHO's 2021 annual guideline is 5 µg/m³, lowered from 10 after evidence showed substantial cardiovascular and respiratory mortality well below the old standard. The U.S. EPA annual NAAQS is 9 µg/m³ (revised in 2024 from 12). Most U.S. cities exceed the WHO guideline. People with cardiovascular disease, asthma, or COPD, and children and older adults, are at greater risk at any given exposure level. Pope & Dockery mortality data →

How do I test my tap water for PFAS and lead?

The most thorough option is a certified laboratory kit: Tap Score's Advanced City Water Test covers 110+ contaminants including PFAS, lead, and disinfection byproducts, and returns a detailed health-context report. Your utility is also required to publish an annual Consumer Confidence Report (CCR) listing tested contaminant levels — though it reflects the treatment plant output, not necessarily your specific tap. A lab test remains the gold standard for confirming what you're actually drinking. See testing kits →

Is indoor air quality worse than outdoor air quality?

Often yes. The EPA estimates Americans spend about 90% of their time indoors, and indoor air can contain 2–5× higher concentrations of some pollutants than outdoor air. Buildings concentrate cooking byproducts, VOCs from furniture and cleaning products, and outdoor PM2.5 that infiltrates through gaps. Modern energy-efficient homes with tight seals can trap these pollutants longer. A continuous air quality monitor (like the Airthings View Plus) will show your actual indoor PM2.5 levels in real time so you can verify whether your purifier is doing its job.
