If you came here looking for a psilocybin version of the MDMA supplement stack, the honest answer is that **most of it does not transfer, because the problem it was built to solve does not exist here.** The MDMA protocol targets oxidative stress, serotonergic terminal damage, and hyperthermia. Psilocin is a direct 5-HT2A receptor agonist rather than a monoamine releaser, so there is no serotonin dump to oxidise anything and no depletion to refill. Pre-loading antioxidants and post-loading 5-HTP around a mushroom trip solves a problem you do not have. What actually helps is a short list, and most of it is not a supplement.

## Quick answers

**Do I need antioxidants before shrooms?** No. The antioxidant rationale comes from oxidative damage generated by MDMA's specific mechanism. Psilocybin does not work that way, so there is nothing to protect.

**Should I take 5-HTP after mushrooms?** No. 5-HTP is a post-MDMA idea aimed at replenishing depleted serotonin, and psilocybin does not deplete serotonin stores.

**What actually reduces mushroom nausea?** An empty stomach is the main answer. Ginger is the one supplement with real trial support behind it for nausea generally ([PMID 16389016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16389016/)).

**Is the Stamets Stack proven?** No. Lion's mane has preclinical nerve growth factor data and one small human trial on its own, but **no controlled study has ever tested the psilocybin plus lion's mane plus niacin combination in people.**

**Is the niacin in that stack safe?** At the 25 to 200 mg doses usually described, documented liver injury is unlikely. The risk shows up when people escalate the dose chasing a flush or buy sustained-release niacin, which is the formulation with the worst hepatotoxicity record ([PMID 1731514](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1731514/)).

## Why the MDMA protocol does not carry over

The [MDMA supplements protocol](/blog/mdma-supplements-protocol.html) exists for specific pharmacological reasons. MDMA reverses the serotonin transporter and forces a massive release of serotonin. Dopamine gets taken up into serotonin terminals during that process and oxidises there, generating reactive oxygen species, usually in a hot room with hours of physical exertion on top. That combination of oxidative stress and hyperthermia is what the R-ALA and vitamin C pre-load targets, based on rat studies where injected antioxidants prevented serotonergic deficits ([PMID 10619665](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10619665/), [PMID 11170222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11170222/)). Serotonin depletion afterwards is what the 5-HTP post-load targets.

Psilocin does none of that. It binds 5-HT2A receptors and activates them directly. It is not a releaser and not a reuptake inhibitor, and it does not raise the serotonin sitting in the synapse, the same pharmacology that makes serotonin toxicity from psychedelics without an MAOI a low-risk scenario ([PMID 34251464](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34251464/)). Nichols' survey in *Pharmacological Reviews* describes classic psychedelics as generally physiologically safe and as not producing dependence or addiction ([PMID 26841800](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26841800/)), and no human study has shown the serotonergic terminal damage from psilocybin that has been argued for heavy MDMA use.

So the neuroprotection framing has nothing to attach to. The supplements are not dangerous, they are aimed at nothing.

## Before: the short list

**A light stomach.** The real nausea intervention. A meta-analysis of therapeutic psilocybin trials found nausea at a relative risk of 8.85 against placebo, more elevated than any other acute adverse effect it measured, with everything resolving inside 48 hours ([PMID 38598236](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38598236/)). Fasting 3 to 4 hours beforehand reduces it and gives a cleaner come-up. A heavy meal pushes onset past 90 minutes, which is where people make the redosing mistake described in our [psilocybin timeline](/blog/how-long-do-shrooms-last.html).

**Ginger, taken beforehand.** The one supplement here with a decent evidence base, though the trials are for surgical and pregnancy nausea rather than psilocybin. A meta-analysis of five RCTs in 363 patients found ginger at 1 g or more reduced postoperative nausea and vomiting, relative risk 0.69 ([PMID 16389016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16389016/)). A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs in 1,278 pregnant women found significant improvement in nausea, though not in vomiting episodes, and no safety signal ([PMID 24642205](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24642205/)). Extrapolating to mushroom nausea is an assumption, but a cheap and low-risk one. [Ginger capsules](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=ginger+capsules+nausea&tag=ravewellness01-20) 30 to 60 minutes before dosing sit better on an empty stomach than tea in volume.

**Water, in normal amounts.** Psilocybin does not carry the hyponatremia risk that MDMA does, so there is no reason to force fluids or to restrict them.

**That is the list.** No antioxidant stack, no amino acids, no nootropics. Preparation for a mushroom session is behavioural, covered in our [safe psilocybin trip guide](/blog/safe-psilocybin-trip-guide.html).

## After: recovery, not neuroprotection

**Electrolytes if you vomited.** Throwing up during the come-up is common and usually resolves as the peak sets in. If it happened more than once, an [electrolyte powder](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=electrolyte+powder+packets&tag=ravewellness01-20) in water replaces what plain water does not. Basic rehydration, not neuroprotection.

**Food, once you want it.** Appetite reliably returns during the descent. Eat.

**Sleep support, if you need it.** Most people can sleep once effects end, unlike after MDMA or LSD, but a late or stimulating session can leave you wired. A meta-analysis of 19 placebo-controlled trials in 1,683 people found melatonin reduced sleep onset latency and improved sleep quality, with effects the authors themselves called modest ([PMID 23691095](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23691095/)). [Low-dose melatonin](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=melatonin+0.5mg+1mg&tag=ravewellness01-20) at 0.5 to 1 mg is the sensible starting point; the 10 mg products exceed what the evidence calls for. [Magnesium glycinate](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=magnesium+glycinate&tag=ravewellness01-20) is a low-risk addition on thinner evidence, and the jaw-clenching rationale that justifies it around MDMA does not apply here, since psilocybin does not cause bruxism.

**Not 5-HTP.** There is nothing to replenish.

## The Stamets Stack, honestly

The Stamets Stack is a microdosing protocol combining a psilocybin microdose (commonly 0.1 g of dried mushroom), lion's mane, and niacin, typically four days on and three off. The proposed logic: lion's mane drives nerve growth factor, psilocybin drives neuroplasticity, and niacin's vasodilation helps distribute the other two.

**Lion's mane on its own has real preclinical data.** Its hericenones and erinacines induce NGF synthesis, shown in human astrocytoma cell culture with confirmation in mouse hippocampus ([PMID 18758067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18758067/)). That is in vitro and rodent work, the bottom rungs of the ladder. There is one small human trial: a double-blind placebo-controlled study in 30 adults with mild cognitive impairment found improved cognitive scores over 16 weeks, the benefit gone four weeks after stopping ([PMID 18844328](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18844328/)). Thirty people is a pilot. [Lion's mane](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=lions+mane+mushroom+supplement&tag=ravewellness01-20) is low-risk and cheap if you want to try it, but **no controlled human trial has ever tested it combined with psilocybin, and the neurogenesis claims made for the stack have not been measured in people.**

**The niacin rationale is not established.** No human data supports the idea that a niacin flush ferries psilocin or lion's mane compounds across the blood-brain barrier. Psilocin crosses without help, and niacin's vasodilation is cutaneous, which is why you feel it in your face and chest.

**The stack also inherits microdosing's own evidence problem.** Placebo-controlled work on microdosing has struggled to separate real effects from expectancy, covered in our [microdosing psilocybin](/blog/microdosing-psilocybin.html) guide.

## Niacin and your liver: the part worth taking seriously

Niacin hepatotoxicity is real, well documented, and both dose- and formulation-dependent. The adult dietary requirement is around 14 to 16 mg a day. Doses above 500 mg daily produce transient, asymptomatic liver enzyme elevations in up to 20% of people, and serious injury becomes common above 3 g a day, the range used for lipid lowering.

**Formulation matters more than dose.** Rader and colleagues found that at doses producing equivalent lipid reductions, hepatic toxicity occurred more frequently with time-release preparations than with unmodified niacin, with liver signs appearing within a week in some patients ([PMID 1731514](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1731514/)). Dalton and Berry documented severe hepatic dysfunction two days after a patient switched from crystalline to sustained-release niacin at the same dose ([PMID 1626557](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1626557/)). Fulminant hepatic failure requiring transplant has been reported.

The doses usually quoted for the Stamets Stack, 100 to 200 mg and sometimes as low as 25 to 50 mg, sit below where liver injury has been documented. Two things move people into the risk zone anyway. The first is escalating the dose to get a flush, since the flush is the point and tolerance to it builds within days of daily use. The second is buying a "time-release" or "flush-free" product, precisely the formulation with the worst record, and taking it four days a week for weeks.

Since the reason for including niacin at all is unsupported, **the reasonable move is to leave it out.** A sudden hot, red, itchy flush arriving 20 minutes into a psychedelic is also easy to misread as something going wrong.

## What to actually avoid

- **Lithium.** The hard stop. In an analysis of 62 reports of lithium plus a classic psychedelic, 47% described a seizure ([PMID 34348413](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34348413/)). Full detail in [LSD and lithium](/blog/lsd-and-lithium.html).
- **MAOIs**, including phenelzine, tranylcypromine, moclobemide, selegiline, linezolid, and harmala alkaloids. These potentiate rather than blunt, unpredictably, and they are the one context where serotonin toxicity risk is genuine.
- **Tramadol.** Serotonergic and independently seizure-lowering.
- **St John's Wort.** A serotonergic herbal supplement people forget counts as a drug interaction, plus broad CYP induction affecting other medications.
- **SSRIs and SNRIs.** Not dangerous so much as disappointing: around half of users report weaker-than-expected effects ([PMID 37291890](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37291890/)). Do not stop your medication to trip, and do not take more to compensate. See [shrooms and antidepressants](/blog/shrooms-and-antidepressants.html).

## Integration beats supplements

What determines how a mushroom session goes is dose, set, setting, and what you do with it afterwards. None of that is purchasable. Antioxidant stacks appeal because they offer something concrete to do about a risk that, for psilocybin, is mostly psychological rather than chemical. The real risk management is dose accuracy, covered in [can you overdose on mushrooms](/blog/can-you-overdose-on-mushrooms.html) and our [psilocybin harm reduction guide](/psilocybin.html), and dropping the dose when you change the delivery method, as in [lemon tek](/blog/lemon-tek-psilocybin.html).

## The bottom line

Before: empty stomach, ginger, water. After: electrolytes if you vomited, food, and sleep support if you need it. Skip the antioxidants, skip the 5-HTP, and skip the niacin. Lion's mane is fine to take if you want to, as long as you know that the combination it is famous for has never been tested in a controlled human study.
