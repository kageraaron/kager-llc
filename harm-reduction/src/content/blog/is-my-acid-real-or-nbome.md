The folk test is real but you should not rely on it. NBOMe compounds are commonly described as bitter or numbing on the tongue, while LSD is usually tasteless, and that difference does exist. It is also subjective, dose-dependent, and it requires holding a suspected NBOMe tab in your mouth, which is the exposure you were trying to avoid. **The test that answers the question is Ehrlich's reagent.** It reacts with indoles, so LSD, DMT and psilocybin all turn it purple, while NBOMe compounds are not indoles and produce no colour change at all. On blotter, no reaction means it is not LSD. Give it up to 20 minutes before you call it, because Ehrlich is slow and reading it early is how people get a false negative.

## Quick answers

**What does Ehrlich do on real LSD?** Turns purple, building gradually. The purple can take several minutes to appear and up to 20 minutes to finish.

**What does NBOMe do on Ehrlich?** Nothing. No purple, no colour change. NBOMe compounds are phenethylamines, not indoles, so the reagent has nothing to react with.

**Can I just taste it?** NBOMe is usually bitter or numbing and LSD is usually tasteless, but taste is subjective, doses vary, and the test itself doses you with the thing you are worried about.

**Does Ehrlich prove it is LSD?** No. It confirms an indole. It cannot separate LSD from 1P-LSD, DMT or other indoles, and it cannot tell you the dose.

## Why the bitter test is not enough

The heuristic came from real reports. People who took NBOMe blotter described a bitter, metallic or numbing sensation consistent enough that it entered scene folklore fast. LSD, by contrast, is active in such tiny quantities that there is almost nothing on the paper to taste.

The problem is what running the test requires. NBOMe absorbs through the oral mucosa, which is its intended route of administration, so by the time you decide it tastes wrong you have taken part of a dose of the compound with the narrow margin.

Taste also fails on its own terms. Blotter paper has a flavour, ink has a flavour, and people's thresholds differ. A low-dose NBOMe tab may not read as strongly bitter, and a tab that tastes of paper tells you nothing. Treat bitterness as a warning that ends the evening, never as clearance.

## Ehrlich's reagent is the actual answer

Ehrlich's reagent contains dimethylaminobenzaldehyde in acid, and it reacts with the indole ring. That single fact does all the work here.

- **LSD**: purple
- **DMT**: purple
- **Psilocybin and psilocin**: purple
- **NBOMe compounds (25I, 25B, 25C and relatives)**: no reaction, no colour change
- **DOx compounds, other phenethylamines**: no reaction

NBOMe compounds are substituted phenethylamines, structurally unrelated to lysergamides ([PMID 32174803](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32174803/)). They have no indole ring, so Ehrlich has nothing to grab. **A blotter tab that produces no colour change on Ehrlich is not LSD.** That negative result is the whole reason to own this reagent.

### Give it 20 minutes

Ehrlich is the slowest reagent in common use. Colour often starts building within a few minutes, but DanceSafe's instructions state it may take up to 20 minutes to react fully. The most common user error is reading it at 60 seconds, seeing nothing, and either binning good acid or, worse, calling the test inconclusive and taking it anyway. Set a timer. ### Technique on blotter

Blotter carries very little material, which is why the technique differs from testing powder.

**Use a corner or a quarter tab.** The reagent needs something to react with, and a tiny snip may not carry enough. A quarter tab in a white [spot plate](/spot-plate) well is a reasonable sacrifice for knowing what you are holding.

**Watch the paper, not the liquid.** The purple develops in and around the wetted paper rather than colouring the whole droplet.

**Ignore the printed dye.** Blotter art bleeds into the reagent and produces colours unrelated to the reaction. Look for a developing purple, not a pink smear from the ink.

**Gels react slowly.** They are much denser than paper, so allow extra time before calling a gel tab negative.

### What Ehrlich cannot tell you

Ehrlich confirms the indole class, nothing narrower. It cannot distinguish LSD from **1P-LSD, 1cP-LSD, ALD-52, DMT or psilocybin**, all of which turn it purple, and it cannot tell you dose, which is what matters most on blotter. A 75 microgram tab and a 300 microgram tab produce the same purple.

Pair it with **Hofmann reagent** for a second data point, since lysergamides drive Hofmann to a deep blue while NBOMe does not. Our guide to [whether you should test LSD](/blog/should-you-test-lsd.html) walks through the full workflow.

## Dose geometry is the real danger

Most drug substitution is a disappointment. This one kills people, and the reason is arithmetic.

LSD is active in the **microgram** range with an unusually wide margin. A review of its pharmacology concludes that LSD is physiologically well tolerated, with the serious risks being psychological and situational rather than toxic ([PMID 19040555](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19040555/)). A case series of accidental overdoses includes a person who insufflated roughly 550 times a normal recreational dose and survived ([PMID 32048609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32048609/)). Taking twice as much LSD as you meant to is a longer, far more intense night. It is rarely a medical emergency by itself.

NBOMe compounds fit on the same square of paper at similar-looking doses and behave nothing like that. They are extremely potent 5-HT2A agonists ([PMID 23473462](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23473462/)), described in the pharmacology literature as highly potent and toxic alternatives to LSD ([PMID 32174803](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32174803/)). Clinical reports document seizures, agitated delirium, hyperthermia, hypertension, rhabdomyolysis and multi-organ failure, including a cluster of hospitalised 25B-NBOMe cases ([PMID 26621342](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26621342/)) and deaths in people who believed they were taking LSD ([PMID 25747271](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25747271/)).

That is the asymmetry. **Two tabs of LSD is a rough night. Two tabs of NBOMe can be a hospital.** The gap between a recreational NBOMe dose and a toxic one is narrow, and blotter is not a precise delivery system: the drop that soaked one square does not carry the same amount as the drop next to it. Even the seller may not know which compound is on the paper, since NBOMes are substituted for each other in the supply ([PMID 25387562](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25387562/)).

## Two other things people mistake for evidence

**Unusually large or thick blotter.** LSD needs almost no space, and the standard quarter-inch square exists because that is all the paper a dose requires. A tab that is noticeably oversized, printed on thick card, or heavy in the hand means someone needed more surface area to carry more material, which is consistent with a compound active at higher amounts than LSD. That is a prompt to test rather than proof, but an unusual format deserves suspicion.

**"It must be real because it was expensive."** Cost tracks scarcity and market position, not identity, and a well-meaning seller a few steps down the chain is passing on what they were told. Nobody in that chain ran an assay.

Same for "everyone else took it and they were fine." Sheets are not homogeneous, and severe NBOMe reactions have hit some people from a sheet while others reported an ordinary trip.

## The bottom line

Bitter or numbing blotter is a genuine warning sign, and it is not a test, because running it means dosing yourself with the compound you are afraid of. Use **Ehrlich's reagent** instead: purple means an indole, consistent with LSD, and **no colour change on blotter means it is not LSD.** Wait the full 20 minutes, test a corner or quarter tab so the reagent has material to work with, and remember that purple confirms a class, never a dose.
