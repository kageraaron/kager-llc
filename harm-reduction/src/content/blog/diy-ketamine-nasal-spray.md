A DIY ketamine nasal spray is a weighed quantity of ketamine dissolved in a measured volume of sterile saline and loaded into a pump bottle. No prescriber, no pharmacy, no label. **Everything the device does for you depends on two numbers you control yourself: milligrams of drug in, millilitres of solvent in.** Get those right and you have the single best argument for a spray, which is that you know what a dose is. Get them wrong, or let them drift, and you have a bottle that lies to you with more confidence than a line ever did.

This post covers the reasoning, the arithmetic and the ways the arithmetic breaks, not a preparation recipe. For how homemade sprays compare to Spravato and compounded telehealth ketamine, see [ketamine nasal spray: Spravato vs at-home vs DIY](/blog/ketamine-nasal-spray.html).

## Quick answers

**What is the actual advantage of a DIY nasal spray?** Dose precision. A weighed amount in a known volume gives a repeatable milligram figure per spray. Eyeballing a line does not.

**How do I work out mg per spray?** Concentration times actuator volume. 500 mg in 5 mL of saline is 100 mg/mL, and a 0.1 mL actuator delivers 10 mg per press.

**Is 0.1 mL per spray a safe assumption?** No. Pumps vary between devices. Measure your own by priming, spraying a counted number of times into a measuring container, and dividing.

**Does an old bottle get weaker?** The opposite. Solvent evaporates and ketamine does not, so an old bottle is a dose-creep risk, not a weak one.

**How long does homemade ketamine solution last?** Nobody can give you a validated number. No published stability study exists for a homemade ketamine nasal solution.

**Does a spray reduce bladder or memory damage?** No. Those track cumulative exposure, not the delivery device.

## The one thing a spray genuinely buys you

Ketamine is a drug where the distance between a light, functional intranasal dose and a full k-hole is often only 50 to 100 mg. That is a narrow window to be guessing at, and a line drawn by eye is a guess: the same person cutting the same powder produces different lines on different nights depending on grind, moisture and how the powder settled.

A solution removes that variability by moving the measurement upstream. You weigh once, dilute once, and every press afterwards inherits that accuracy.

The maths is simple enough to do in your head:

- 500 mg of ketamine dissolved into 5 mL of sterile saline gives **100 mg/mL**.
- A pump that delivers **0.1 mL** per actuation therefore gives **10 mg per spray**.
- Two sprays is 20 mg, four is 40 mg, and you can count rather than estimate.

Change either input and the whole scale shifts. 250 mg in 5 mL is 50 mg/mL, so the same pump gives 5 mg per spray. Nothing is magic about a particular concentration; what matters is knowing which one you made, and writing it on the bottle rather than trusting your memory a fortnight later.

None of this works without an accurate weight at the start. A [milligram scale](/mg-scale) is doing the real work in this whole exercise, because the spray is only ever as accurate as the weighing that preceded it. A jewellery scale that reads to 0.1 g cannot resolve the difference between 400 mg and 500 mg, which is a 25% error baked into every dose you take from that bottle. A [DIY nasal spray kit](/nasal-spray-kit) gets you a bottle intended for nasal use rather than a repurposed container. Both are from DanceSafe, a nonprofit.

## Failure mode one: assuming your actuator volume

The 0.1 mL figure quoted everywhere is typical, not universal. Spray pumps are manufactured to different specifications, and a bottle you bought from a general supplier may deliver noticeably more or less per press. If you do not know what your own pump actually puts out, the milligrams-per-spray number is guesswork wearing a lab coat, and the precision you thought you bought is imaginary.

Measuring it is straightforward. Prime the pump until it sprays consistently, then actuate it a counted number of times into a small measuring container, and divide the total volume collected by the number of sprays. Do it once per bottle design, with plain saline before any drug goes in, and your dose figure becomes a measurement rather than an assumption.

## Failure mode two: evaporation concentrates the solution

This is the one that catches people out, because it runs against intuition. When a bottle sits around with an imperfect seal and some of the solvent evaporates, **the ketamine does not evaporate with it**. The drug stays in the bottle. The liquid it is dissolved in gets smaller. Same milligrams, fewer millilitres, so the concentration goes up.

The practical consequence is that an old, half-forgotten bottle at the back of a drawer is a **dose-creep risk, not a weak one**. Every spray from it delivers more than it did the day you mixed it, and you cannot know how much more without remeasuring the remaining volume. Assuming an old bottle has gone stale and lost potency is backwards, and it points you toward taking more.

## Shelf life: an honest non-answer

There is no validated shelf life for a homemade ketamine nasal solution, because **no published stability study has been done on one**. Anyone quoting you a confident number of weeks is inventing it. That is not a gap this post can close, and pretending otherwise would be worse than saying it plainly.

## Contamination is the real case for small fresh batches

The microbial side is better established than the chemical stability side, and it is the actual reason to make small batches you will get through rather than a large one you nurse for months.

- **There is no preservative in a DIY solution.** Commercial nasal products often avoid preservatives as well, because they irritate the nasal mucosa, which is precisely why preservative-free nasal formulations carry a limited in-use life once opened ([PMID 15018996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15018996/)).
- **Contaminated nasal spray is a documented outbreak source, not a hypothetical.** The CDC investigated a hospital outbreak of *Burkholderia cepacia* traced to contaminated albuterol and nasal spray ([PMID 17099009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17099009/)).
- **The bottle reinoculates itself.** The spray tip goes into your nose, picks up whatever is living there, and returns to the reservoir. Every use is a small deposit into the bottle.

Use sterile saline rather than tap water. Replace a bottle rather than topping it up, since topping up means a fresh batch immediately inherits whatever the old one accumulated.

**Do not share the bottle.** A spray tip that has been inside somebody else's nose carries the same blood-contact logic as a shared straw, and nasal membranes bleed more readily than people think. That is covered in [sharing straws and hepatitis C](/blog/sharing-straws-hepatitis-c.html).

## What a spray does not do

A measured solution is a genuine improvement on an unmeasured line. It is a **dosing improvement, not a safety upgrade to the drug**, and the long-term harms of ketamine track cumulative exposure and frequency rather than the device it came from.

- **It does not reduce bladder risk.** Ketamine-associated uropathy scales with how much and how often, not how it was delivered. See [ketamine bladder damage](/blog/ketamine-bladder-damage.html).
- **It does not reduce cognitive risk.** Memory and executive function deficits follow heavy, frequent use. See [does ketamine cause brain damage](/blog/does-ketamine-cause-brain-damage.html).
- **It does not reduce dependence risk.** A pocketable, pre-dosed device arguably makes redosing easier and less deliberate, which is the wrong direction. See [is ketamine addictive](/blog/is-ketamine-addictive.html).
- **It does not tell you what the powder was.** A spray dilutes an unknown into a precisely measured unknown. Reagent test and fentanyl strip before anything goes into solution; a [ketamine testing kit](/ketamine-kit) is the baseline.

Worth knowing too that street ketamine is racemic, a 50/50 mix of two mirror-image forms, so it is not the same thing as the pure esketamine in the FDA-approved product. That distinction is explained in [R vs S ketamine](/blog/r-vs-s-ketamine.html).

If frequency is creeping up, no amount of dosing accuracy addresses that. Dose control and use frequency are separate problems, and only one of them is solved by a spray bottle. Route comparisons, k-hole safety and bladder protection are all in our [ketamine harm reduction guide](/ketamine.html).
