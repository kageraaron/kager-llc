Probably, but nobody has proven it. Hepatitis C transmission from sharing a snorting straw is biologically plausible and supported by real epidemiological signals, and no study has ever isolated straw-sharing as the route of infection in a person. Rehab sites state it as settled fact, forums call it a myth, and both are overstating what is known. The uncertainty does not matter for what you should do: using your own tube costs nothing, so this is a risk you act on without waiting for proof.

## Quick answers

**Can hepatitis C survive on a straw?** Yes. HCV in dried plasma stayed infectious for at least 16 hours at room temperature (PMID 17464909), and up to 5 days on a serum-coated surface (PMID 22013220). A straw used an hour ago sits inside that window.

**Is there blood on snorting equipment?** Often. Of 54 straws confiscated by law enforcement, 13 (24%) tested positive for human blood (PMID 27400008), and HCV RNA has been recovered from the nasal secretions and snorting implements of infected users (PMID 18764772).

**Has anyone proven you can catch hep C from a straw?** No. Every study has a confound: most people who snort drugs have also injected or had other exposures.

**Is a rolled banknote worse than a straw?** Yes. Bills pass through many hands, hold residue in the fibers, and cannot be cleaned. A [personal reusable tube](/crisp-tube) is a clear improvement.

**If I have shared straws, what should I do?** Get a hepatitis C antibody test. HCV is curable with direct-acting antivirals, so testing leads to treatment, not dread.

## The mechanism: why this is even a plausible question

Hepatitis C is blood-borne. For a straw to transmit it, three things must line up: blood on the equipment, virus surviving in that blood, and a route into the next person's bloodstream. All three are documented.

**The nose bleeds.** Stimulants constrict the vessels feeding the nasal lining, starving that tissue of oxygen, and the powder itself is abrasive. Regular users develop a fragile, easily bleeding mucosa long before anything dramatic like a septal perforation appears, a progression our [cocaine harm reduction guide](/blog/cocaine-harm-reduction.html) covers in detail. Visible nosebleeds are not the threshold: micro-abrasions that never produce a drip are enough to put blood on a tube and let virus in at the other end.

**Virus is present in nasal fluid, not just blood.** McMahon and colleagues first detected HCV RNA in an intranasal drug user's nasal secretions in 2004, in the one participant of five with the highest viral load (PMID 15132748). A larger 2008 study confirmed blood and HCV RNA in the nasal secretions and snorting implements of infected users at a New York clinic (PMID 18764772).

**The virus is durable.** It survives drying, from 16 hours in plasma (PMID 17464909) to 5 days on a serum-coated surface (PMID 22013220), easily covering a tube passed around a table over one night.

Every link has evidence. What is missing is a study that watched all three happen in one person.

## What the evidence does and does not establish

Two studies do most of the work, failing in opposite directions.

**Fernandez et al., 2016, *Obstetrics and Gynecology*.** 189 HCV-infected pregnant women were surveyed about transmission risks. 178 (94%) reported snorting drugs, and of those, 164 (92%) reported sharing straws. Only 136 (72%) reported intravenous drug use, making shared snorting equipment significantly more common than shared injection equipment (P<.001). The authors also tested straws confiscated by law enforcement from unrelated people, finding human blood on 24% (PMID 27400008).

**The limitation, stated plainly: 72% of that cohort also injected.** Injection is a far more efficient route for HCV, so for most of these women the straw is not the likeliest explanation, and the study cannot separate the two. A subgroup of 29 (15%) reported straw-sharing and denied every risk factor except sexual contact, which is as close as the paper gets to isolation.

**Simmons et al., 2022, *Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs*.** This one fixes that flaw. It analyzed 10,106 people from the 2011 to 2018 NHANES survey, **restricted to people who had never injected an illicit substance**. Cocaine use was associated with **4.48 times the adjusted odds of ever having HCV (95% CI 2.36 to 8.50)**, controlling for age, race, sex, education, income, and immigrant status (PMID 35254242).

Removing injection as a confounder is the strongest design available, and a fourfold association in never-injectors is hard to explain away. But it measures *cocaine use*, not *straw-sharing*. People who use cocaine differ from those who do not in ways those covariates miss: sexual networks, tattooing, incarceration. Residual confounding is real.

A 2003 review by McMahon and Tortu reached the same verdict, and little has changed since: some epidemiological support, most studies methodologically limited, biology that does not refute intranasal transmission (PMID 14986874).

**The honest summary:** plausible mechanism, confirmed contamination of real equipment, a solid signal in never-injectors, no proof of the route itself. Not a myth. Not a settled fact.

## Why the uncertainty does not change the advice

Uncertainty matters when acting on it is expensive. Here it is not. Not sharing costs a few dollars and slightly more coordination; the downside if the risk is real is a chronic liver infection. When one side of a decision is nearly free and the other is a blood-borne virus, you act on plausible risk rather than wait for proof. Same reasoning as not sharing a razor.

## Rolled banknotes are a specifically bad choice

Bills are the default improvised tube and the worst option:

- **They circulate.** A note has been handled by strangers, sat in tills and pockets, and been rolled by other people.
- **Fibers hold residue.** Currency is woven cotton and linen, not a smooth surface. It absorbs and retains biological material, and cannot be cleaned.
- **The edges are sharp.** A tightly rolled bill has a stiff cut edge that scrapes the nasal lining, as do cut straws and rolled cards. That abrasion causes the bleeding that creates the risk.
- **They get passed by default.** Nobody treats a bill as personal equipment, so it circulates without anyone deciding to share.

## Safer snorting equipment: the practical version

None of this makes snorting safe. It removes one avoidable harm.

- **One tube per person, never passed.** Carry [a personal reusable tube](/crisp-tube). DanceSafe sells these explicitly as a hepatitis C prevention measure, and as a nonprofit it also runs free drug checking at events.
- **Label or colour-code in a group.** The failure mode is not deciding to share, it is tubes getting mixed up on a table. Marker or tape solves it.
- **No sharp or cut edges.** Skip cut straws, rolled cards, and jagged pen barrels. Use smooth, rounded ends.
- **Wash reusable tubes.** Warm water and soap, dried fully. Being cleanable is the main advantage over paper.
- **Do not share the surface or the card.** Blood transfers to the plate and the chopping card, not just the tube.
- **Saline rinse and recovery time.** Less mucosal damage means less bleeding means less to transmit. See the nasal care section of our [cocaine harm reduction guide](/blog/cocaine-harm-reduction.html).

The same logic covers other routes: pipes burn and crack the lips, which is why personal mouthpieces are the standard answer for a shared stem.

## If you have shared equipment, get tested

This part should defuse the anxiety rather than feed it. Hepatitis C is curable. A meta-analysis of 12 studies covering 711 people who use drugs found a pooled sustained virological response of 89.8% (95% CI 85.9 to 92.7) on one direct-acting antiviral regimen (PMID 34315488), and newer pan-genotypic regimens do better. Treatment is usually 8 to 12 weeks of oral tablets.

Testing starts with an antibody test, available at most clinics, many syringe service programs, and by mail. A positive result is followed by an RNA test, since some people clear the virus on their own. Antibodies take weeks to appear, so a test immediately after an incident can miss it. The realistic move for anyone who has shared equipment over the years is one test, not worry.
