**Yes, and not because LSD is hard on your body. Because what gets sold as LSD sometimes is not LSD.** Blotter, liquid and tabs are occasionally NBOMe compounds, which look identical on paper and can cause seizures, severe hyperthermia and death at doses a single square holds easily.

An Ehrlich reagent test cannot confirm you have LSD. What it can do is rule out the substitute that kills. **If the paper does not react with Ehrlich, do not take it.** That is two minutes of work and it is the highest-value thing you can do before dosing.

## LSD is forgiving. "Acid" might not be.

LSD is active in micrograms. A normal dose is 75 to 150 of them, an almost invisible quantity. That potency is precisely why adulteration is invisible: there is so little active material on a tab that swapping in a different microgram-scale chemical changes nothing you could see, taste or feel.

Its own toxicity is remarkably low. A 2020 case series in the *Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs* documented three accidental massive overdoses, including a woman who insufflated an estimated 55 mg of pure LSD, roughly 550 times a recreational dose, and survived without lasting physical harm. The real risks of genuine LSD are psychological and behavioural, not organ toxicity. For the interaction that genuinely does cause seizures, see [LSD and lithium](/blog/lsd-and-lithium.html).

So if you have LSD, you are in decent shape. The question is whether you have LSD.

## NBOMe is the substitute that kills

The 25x-NBOMe family, 25I, 25B and 25C, appeared as research chemicals sold on blotter and passed off, knowingly or otherwise, as acid. They are potent 5-HT2A agonists, so a low dose produces psychedelic effects that someone without much experience could mistake for LSD.

What is not similar is the safety margin. NBOMe compounds have a steep dose-response curve and real physical toxicity: severe agitation and delirium, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, clonus and tonic-clonic seizures, rhabdomyolysis with the kidney failure that follows it, and death.

A 2015 case series in the *Journal of Analytical Toxicology* documented two fatal intoxications in Indiana teenagers tied to 25B-NBOMe and 25I-NBOMe. A 2013 report in *Clinical Toxicology* describes life-threatening 25I-NBOMe intoxication, and an analytically confirmed case describes a suicide attempt in someone who believed they had taken good acid ([PMC4220298](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4220298/)).

There is a folk rule worth knowing: **if it's bitter, it's a spitter.** Real LSD is tasteless. NBOMe is noticeably bitter and often numbs the tongue. A tab that tastes like anything is a warning. But that is a backstop, not a test, and it involves putting an unknown chemical in your mouth to find out.

## How the Ehrlich test works

Ehrlich reagent reacts with the **indole ring** that LSD contains. NBOMe compounds are phenethylamines with no indole, so they do not react at all. That chemical difference is the entire reason this test is worth doing.

1. **Cut a small corner** off the tab, about a quarter. You do not need the whole thing.
2. **Put it on a white ceramic surface** or the test tray, or straight into an Ehrlich tube if your kit uses that format.
3. **Add one or two drops** of reagent directly onto the paper.
4. **Wait two to five minutes.** An indole turns the spot **purple through to pink or magenta**. The reaction can be slow, so do not call it early.
5. **Read it under white light** against the kit's chart.

**No colour change means no indole, which means it is not LSD.** Do not take it.

## What the test cannot tell you

A purple reaction is a good sign, not a green light. Being clear about the limits is what makes the test worth trusting at all.

**It does not identify LSD specifically.** Other indoles also turn Ehrlich purple. The harm reduction logic is that NBOMe, the main lethal substitute, is not an indole, so a clean purple makes NBOMe very unlikely. That is the value. It is not a positive ID.

**It does not detect NBOMe.** A blank result is informative because it means "not LSD," but Ehrlich cannot tell you what the non-reacting substance actually is. A dedicated 25-NBOMe colour spot test, validated in 2021 in *Drug Testing and Analysis*, or mass spectrometry, is what answers that.

**It cannot measure dose.** Reagents detect presence, not potency. A genuine tab can still be far stronger than you expected.

**One reagent is one data point.** Colour tests depend on a person reading a colour correctly, and results can be ambiguous. Hofmann reagent, which turns blue specifically for LSD, narrows it further when used alongside Ehrlich.

If you are at an event running a [drug checking service](/blog/drug-checking-services-festivals.html) with spectrometry, that beats any colorimetric kit by a wide margin. For everyone else, Ehrlich at home is the realistic baseline.

Two checks catch most of the real risk here: no reaction means do not dose, and a bitter or numbing tab means do not dose. Neither is complicated, and both take less time than the drive to the party.
