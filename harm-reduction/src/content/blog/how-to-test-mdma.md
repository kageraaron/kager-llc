**Two reagents and a fentanyl strip take about five minutes and rule out most of the ways a night goes badly wrong.** They will not tell you the dose, and they cannot promise purity, but they catch the substitutions that put people in an ambulance.

Street MDMA is often not MDMA. It gets sold as methamphetamine, as synthetic cathinones, as caffeine and filler, and sometimes as nothing psychoactive at all. Fentanyl contamination of MDMA is less common than in the opioid or cocaine supply, but drug checking services across North America and Europe have confirmed it, and it has killed people.

## Marquis first, Simon's second

**Marquis** reacts with phenethylamines, the chemical family MDMA belongs to. It is the screening test.

| Substance | Marquis color |
|-----------|---------------|
| **MDMA** | **Purple to black** |
| MDA | Purple to black (same) |
| Methamphetamine | Orange to brown |
| Amphetamine | Orange to brown |
| Ketamine | No reaction, stays clear or yellow |
| DXM | Gray to black |

If it does not go purple to black, MDMA is not the main thing in your sample. That is the single most useful result a reagent gives you, and it is why Marquis goes first.

**Simon's** answers the question Marquis leaves open. MDMA and MDA both go purple to black, so Marquis cannot tell them apart. Simon's, which comes in two parts, turns **blue** for MDMA and does nothing for MDA. That distinction matters at the dosing stage: MDA runs longer and has a different risk profile, so knowing which one you are holding changes how you plan the night. Our [MDMA harm reduction guide](/mdma.html) has the dosing.

**Mecke** is worth adding when Marquis gave you something ambiguous or unexpected. MDMA turns it blue-green to black, and it picks up some cathinones the first two miss.

## Running the test

You need the kit, a white ceramic plate, something to scrape with, and decent light.

Scrape about 10 mg onto the plate, a match head or a grain of rice, and no more. Add one or two drops of reagent. Watch the first 30 to 60 seconds, because that is the window where the color means something; every reagent keeps darkening afterwards regardless of what it touched. Compare against the chart in white light, photograph it if you want a record, and rinse the plate before the next reagent.

## Fentanyl strips need double the water

Fentanyl test strips are immunoassays built for urine that also work on dissolved drug samples, and there is published support for their use as a harm reduction tool. MDMA has one specific quirk with them: at high concentration it can trigger a **false positive**.

The fix is dilution, and for MDMA the standard volume is not enough. Use **10 mL of water, two teaspoons, per 10 mg of sample**, which is double what you would use for most drugs and is what DanceSafe specifies for MDMA and methamphetamine. Work on residue testing supports the larger volume as a margin against false positives. If you are unsure, use more water, never less.

1. Dissolve about 10 mg in 10 mL of water.
2. Dip the strip for 15 seconds.
3. Lay it flat and wait 2 to 5 minutes.
4. **Two lines is NEGATIVE, no fentanyl detected. One line is POSITIVE, fentanyl detected.**
5. No lines at all means the test failed. Run a fresh strip.

A positive means fentanyl is in there. Do not use the sample. The full procedure is in our [fentanyl test strip guide](/blog/how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips.html), and [strips are sold separately](/fentanyl-strips) from reagent kits because no reagent detects fentanyl.

## What a color test cannot tell you

Be clear-eyed about this part, because overconfidence in a reagent result is its own hazard. A 2003 paper in *Pharmacotherapy* asked directly whether ecstasy test kits were harm reduction or harm induction, and the concern it raised is still the right one: a reagent tells you what is dominant in the sample, not what is safe.

**Dose and purity are invisible.** A sample that gives a textbook purple-to-black could be 80 mg or 250 mg. Reagents report presence, never quantity, which is why starting low still applies after a clean test.

**Minor adulterants hide.** Colorimetric tests read the dominant compound. Something present at low concentration can sit under the reaction entirely.

**Some substances mimic the reaction.** Color is suggestive, not definitive. FTIR spectroscopy or mass spectrometry at a drug checking service is the real answer, and DanceSafe runs checking at many large events. If there is a service on site, use it; it will tell you far more than a drop of acid on a plate.

Most people do none of this. A survey of the electronic dance music scene found only a minority test regularly, while checking services keep turning up substances nobody expected to find.
