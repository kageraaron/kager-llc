Yes, reagent test kits expire, and a degraded reagent is more dangerous than no reagent, because it still produces a color and you will still believe it. DanceSafe states that **most reagents have a shelf life of at least a year if stored in a refrigerator or freezer**, and that keeping them out of heat and sunlight is what makes them last. The failure mode is not an old bottle visibly dying. It is a bottle that reacts weakly, or slowly, or produces a muddy color you read as the wrong substance, and then you dose on a result that was never valid.

## Quick answers

**Do reagents have an expiration date?** Rarely a printed one. DanceSafe's guidance is that most last at least a year refrigerated or frozen. Heat and sunlight shorten that considerably.

**Is a darkened bottle ruined?** No. Marquis and Mecke gradually darken and stay usable. DanceSafe's cutoff is practical: the reagent is done when the liquid is too dark to see the color reaction against. Mandelin turning cloudy yellow at three to four weeks is also normal.

**How should I store them?** Tightly capped, upright, cool, dark, refrigerated or frozen. Never in a hot car or a bag in the sun.

**When should I replace a bottle?** When the blank is too dark to read a reaction against, when reactions come out weak or slow, or when a known sample gives the wrong color.

## Why reagents degrade in the first place

Reagents are aggressive chemistry in a small bottle, and the reactivity that makes them useful makes them fragile. Marquis is formaldehyde in concentrated sulfuric acid. Froehde is molybdic acid in concentrated sulfuric acid, Mandelin is ammonium vanadate in the same. The pattern holds across most of the chart: a reactive component carried in a strongly acidic medium. Three things act on that over time.

- **Water absorption**: concentrated sulfuric acid is hygroscopic, so it pulls water vapor from the air. Every loose cap dilutes it slightly, and diluted acid drives the color-forming reaction less strongly, so the color comes out fainter and slower.
- **Oxidation**: formaldehyde in Marquis oxidizes over time, and oxidizing reagents react slowly with whatever is available. The bottle darkens as products accumulate.
- **Heat and light**: both accelerate everything above. A reagent that would last a year in a fridge can be visibly changed after one hot weekend in a car.

None of this announces itself. The bottle does not curdle or separate, it just gets less able to do its job.

## What a degraded reagent looks like

This is more nuanced than "yellow means throw it out," and the nuance keeps you from binning a good bottle or trusting a dead one.

**Some darkening is expected.** DanceSafe's instructions say Marquis and Mecke gradually darken with age and remain usable. Fresh Marquis is clear to very pale straw, and over months it drifts through yellow toward amber. That drift alone is not proof it is finished. Mandelin is a special case: it starts light orange and turns cloudy yellow at three to four weeks, which is characteristic, not a defect.

**The real cutoff is readability.** A reagent is expired when the liquid is too dark to see the color reaction happening in it. If your Marquis is already deep amber or brown in the bottle, a purple-to-black MDMA reaction and a brown methamphetamine reaction look like the same dark smear, and the test distinguishes nothing.

**Speed and intensity matter as much as hue.** A fresh reagent produces its color fast and strongly. A tired one gives a pale version, or takes a minute, or fades. If a reaction you have run many times looks washed out, suspect the bottle before the sample.

## The failure mode that actually hurts people

A dead flashlight tells you it is dead. A dead reagent does not. It still drips, still changes color, still gives an answer.

- **A weak reaction read as no reaction.** You get a faint smudge, conclude nothing reactive is present, and have cleared a sample you never really tested.
- **A muddy color read as the wrong substance.** Marquis separates MDMA (purple to black) from methamphetamine and amphetamine (orange to brown) by hue. A degraded reagent shifts every result toward brown, so real MDMA can read as amphetamine, or come out ambiguous enough that you talk yourself into the answer you wanted. Our guide to [reading a Marquis result](/blog/how-to-read-a-marquis-reagent-result.html) covers the full color set.

Skipping a test leaves you appropriately uncertain. Running a bad test leaves you confidently wrong, and confidence changes behavior.

## Storage that actually extends shelf life

DanceSafe's guidance is short: keep reagents out of heat and sunlight, a freezer is ideal, a fridge works too. The practical version:

- **Refrigerate or freeze.** The highest-impact step. A drawer in a cool interior room is second best. A windowsill, a car, or the top of a fridge (which is warm) is not storage.
- **Cap tightly, every time.** A loose cap is a slow leak in the other direction.
- **Store upright and dark.** A tipped dropper bottle leaks corrosive acid. The original box or a closed cupboard is fine, a counter in daylight is not.
- **Never touch the bottle tip to a sample.** That contaminates the whole bottle and ends its life instantly. Drip from above, never the reverse.
- **Bring only what you need to an event.** A festival bag in the sun is hot, bright and shaken. Bring the one or two bottles you will use, keep them shaded, leave the rest in the fridge.
- **Let cold bottles warm up first.** Cold reagent reacts more slowly and can read as weak.

## How to sanity-check a bottle before you trust it

Two checks, both fast, both worth doing before a test you plan to act on.

**1. Look at the blank.** Put one drop of reagent on clean white ceramic with no sample in it. The blank shows the starting color and gives you something to compare the reacted drop against. Run one alongside every test. If it is dark enough that you would struggle to see a color change inside it, the bottle is finished.

**2. Test something you already know.** The strongest check available outside a lab. If you have a portion of something that previously gave a clean textbook result with this reagent, run it. Fresh Marquis on MDMA goes purple to black fast and unmistakably. If that known sample now gives a slow, pale or off-hue version of what it gave before, the reagent has degraded. An old bottle producing a strong, fast, textbook color is probably still working. One producing something ambiguous has told you nothing, and "ambiguous" is not a result to dose on.

## When to replace

- **The blank is too dark to read a reaction against.** DanceSafe's own endpoint, and the clearest signal you will get.
- **A known sample gives a wrong or badly weakened result.**
- **The bottle has been through real heat.** A summer in a car, a weekend in a hot tent. It may still work, but you no longer know that it does.
- **The dropper tip has touched a sample.** The bottle is contaminated.
- **It has sat at room temperature for a long stretch.** The at-least-a-year figure is tied to cold storage, and a warm bottle has no comparable guarantee.

Reagents are consumable supplies, and one bottle runs [50 to 75 tests](/blog/how-many-tests-per-reagent-kit.html), so replacement works out to far less per test than people assume. Dispose of old reagents at a hazardous waste facility, not down a drain. They are concentrated acid.

## The takeaway

Reagents degrade quietly, and the degradation shows up as a misleading result rather than a dead test. Store them capped, upright, cool and dark, run a blank beside every test, and replace a bottle once it is too dark to read a reaction in or a known sample stops behaving.
