**You need a milligram scale if you take powders dosed in tens or hundreds of milligrams, and you do not need one at all if your substance is dosed in micrograms.** That split covers most of the decision. A scale is the right tool for MDMA, ketamine, 2C-B and ground mushrooms, where the numbers that matter sit above what a consumer scale can resolve. It is the wrong tool for LSD and anything active below about a milligram, where the honest answer is volumetric dosing rather than better hardware. And a scale answers exactly one question, "how much," never "what."

## Quick answers

**Is a 0.001 g scale accurate to 1 mg?** No. Readability is what the display shows; accuracy is how close that number is to reality. A budget 0.001 g scale is genuinely unreliable below roughly 10 to 20 mg, no matter how many decimal places it prints.

**Do I need calibration weights?** Yes, and they are not optional. These scales drift with use, temperature and transport, and they frequently arrive uncalibrated. An uncalibrated milligram scale is a random number generator with a nice display.

**Can I weigh an LSD dose?** No. LSD is active at around 100 micrograms, which is 0.1 mg, far below the point where a consumer scale still means anything. See [our LSD guide](/lsd.html). Microgram substances need **volumetric dosing** instead.

**Does a scale replace testing?** Never. A scale answers "how much." A reagent kit answers "what," and only partially. You need both, and [our test kit comparison](/which-test-kit.html) covers which one fits your substance.

## Readability is not accuracy

This is the single most important thing about milligram scales, and product listings work hard to obscure it.

**Readability** is the smallest increment the display can show. A scale advertised as "0.001 g" has 1 mg readability. That is a statement about the screen, not about the measurement.

**Accuracy** is how close the displayed number is to the true mass. On consumer milligram scales, accuracy is worse than readability, often several times worse at the bottom of the range. Load cells have a noise floor, and near that floor a scale will happily show you three decimal places of invented precision. Put the same 5 mg of material on and off a budget scale ten times and you will get a spread of readings, all displayed to the milligram, none of them trustworthy.

The practical rule: **treat a consumer milligram scale as unreliable below roughly 10 to 20 mg.** Above that, in the tens and hundreds of milligrams, it becomes a genuinely useful instrument. Below it, you are reading noise. That is not an argument against buying one, it is an argument for knowing which questions yours can answer.

## What a scale is actually good at

**MDMA.** The range cited across harm reduction organisations is **75 to 125 mg** orally, with 1 to 1.5 mg/kg capped at 120 mg as the common weight-based heuristic. No trial has tested the weight-based rule. The controlled clinical numbers come from the MAPS Phase 3 PTSD trials, which used 80 mg as the starting dose and 120 mg as the higher therapeutic dose under medical supervision ([PMID 33972795](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33972795/)). All of those numbers sit far above a scale's noise floor, which makes MDMA the ideal case. Our [MDMA dosing guide](/blog/mdma-dosing-guide.html) covers the rest.

**Ketamine.** The reason to weigh here is density. A milligram is a milligram whether the material is coarse shards or fine dust, but fine powder packs far more densely, so **the same visually sized line holds meaningfully more drug**. That is the ordinary explanation for a batch that "hit way harder," and it is invisible by eye. More in [R vs S ketamine](/blog/r-vs-s-ketamine.html).

**Ground mushrooms.** The gap between 0.1 g and 0.3 g of powder is invisible once material is ground, and microdosing is entirely about staying under a threshold you cannot see. Grind a batch evenly first, since a single cap is not representative. See [microdosing psilocybin](/blog/microdosing-psilocybin.html).

## The 2C-B and dissociative case: where weighing matters most

Substances active in the low tens of milligrams are where eyeballing fails worst.

For 2C-B, the range cited across harm reduction organisations puts a common oral dose at roughly 15 to 25 mg, with 25 mg and above described as strong. No controlled trial has established these figures. In absolute terms, the distance between a comfortable evening and an overwhelming one can be **5 to 10 mg of powder**, a difference you cannot see on a spoon or a folded paper, and density compounds it.

This is where a scale earns its keep completely: above the noise floor, so the reading is meaningful, and steep enough on the dose-response curve that a 10 mg error changes the night. Dissociatives sit in similar territory. More in [our 2C-B guide](/2cb.html).

A [micro scoop](/micro-scoop) helps here, but understand its job. It is a transfer tool for moving small amounts of powder onto the pan without spilling or static-flinging it, not a measuring device. Scoop, weigh, adjust, weigh again.

## Calibration, and the technique that changes the reading

**Calibrate it, then recalibrate it.** Milligram scales drift, and they often ship out of calibration. Run the routine when it arrives, again if it has been knocked or travelled, and periodically after that. This needs a calibration weight of the correct mass, usually 20 g or 50 g depending on the model. DanceSafe does not sell calibration weights, so you will need to source a set separately from a lab supply or scale retailer. Do not skip this because the scale "seems fine."

Technique matters at these masses:

- **Level, solid surface**: not a bed, not a couch arm, not your lap. Use the bubble level if there is one.
- **No draught**: a ceiling fan, open window or air vent moves the reading by several milligrams. Close the draught shield.
- **Static**: dry air plus a plastic boat plus fine powder equals powder that jumps. Wipe the boat, or use paper.
- **Weighing paper or a boat, then tare**: never put powder straight on the pan. Place the paper, press tare, then add material.
- **Let it settle**: wait for the reading to stop moving before you trust it.
- **Temperature**: let the scale reach room temperature first. A cold scale drifts as it warms.

## Volumetric dosing: when the scale cannot help

For anything active in micrograms, no consumer scale works, and a more expensive one does not fix it. LSD at around 100 micrograms is 0.1 mg, well inside the range where the display is showing you noise.

The correct method is **volumetric dosing**: dissolve a known quantity of substance in a known volume of liquid, then measure the liquid instead of the powder. Measuring 1 mL of solution is repeatable in a way that measuring 100 micrograms of solid never will be. A [graduated pipette](/pipette) handles the liquid half. The catch is that you still need an accurate starting quantity, which means beginning from a source of known content.

## When you genuinely do not need one

**Pressed pills of unknown content.** Weighing a tablet gives the combined mass of active ingredient, binder and filler in unknown proportions. A 300 mg pill might hold 80 mg of MDMA or 220 mg, and the scale cannot tell you which. Split-and-wait beats weighing here.

**Blotter, tabs and anything sold by unit.** The scale has nothing to measure.

**As a substitute for identifying your substance.** This is the mistake that matters: **a scale answers "how much" and never "what."** A precisely weighed 90 mg of an unidentified white powder is still an unidentified white powder. Reagent testing and fentanyl strips come first, and [our reagent kit coverage guide](/blog/how-many-tests-per-reagent-kit.html) explains how many tests a kit actually gives you.

## The takeaway

If you use powders dosed above about 20 mg, a [milligram scale](/mg-scale) is one of the highest-value harm reduction purchases available, and the DanceSafe one funds a nonprofit that runs free drug checking at events. Look for 0.001 g readability, a draught shield and a stated calibration procedure, and buy calibration weights separately. Then hold two facts at once: it is precise enough for the doses most people take, and not precise enough for single milligrams or micrograms.
