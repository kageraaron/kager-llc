A Mecke reagent test tells you which chemical family your sample belongs to by the color it turns in the first 45 to 60 seconds. **MDMA and MDA both go very dark, essentially black; heroin and related opiates turn blue-green; amphetamine, cocaine, ketamine, and LSD produce no reaction at all.** Mecke is a useful second or third reagent because it separates the methylenedioxy compounds from the amphetamine-type stimulants in a single glance, and because it is one of the classic opiate reagents. It cannot tell MDMA from MDA, it cannot measure dose or purity, and it cannot detect fentanyl. This guide is the color chart plus the mistakes that cause misreads.

## Quick answers

**What color is MDMA on a Mecke test?** Very dark, essentially black. The reaction is fast and the finished color on DanceSafe's current chart is a near-black dot. Charts often describe it passing through blue or blue-green on the way there, so a dark blue-black is the same expected result.

**What does blue-green mean on a Mecke test?** Blue-green points to the opiate family. DanceSafe's chart shows heroin as a teal to blue-green reaction with a dark center, and published reference tables list morphine and codeine as a very dark bluish green.

**What does no reaction mean?** For Mecke, "no reaction" is real information. Amphetamine, cocaine, ketamine, LSD, mephedrone, and PMMA are all documented as no reaction, so a sample that does nothing under Mecke is telling you it does not contain a methylenedioxy compound or an opiate.

**Can Mecke tell MDMA from MDA?** No. Both go near-black and look identical. Only [Simon's reagent](/blog/simons-reagent-color-chart.html) separates them, which is why Simon's is the second bottle in every MDMA workflow.

**Can Mecke detect fentanyl?** No. No reagent can. Fentanyl can be present even when the color looks exactly right, so always also use a [fentanyl test strip](/blog/how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips.html).

## The Mecke color chart

Mecke starts out clear and gradually darkens in the bottle over weeks or months, which is normal. Read the reaction on a white ceramic or hard plastic surface within the first 45 to 60 seconds, because every reagent eventually drifts to brown or black on its own and a slow darkening after several minutes is not a real result.

| Substance | Mecke color |
|-----------|-------------|
| **MDMA** | **Very dark blue-black to black** |
| **MDA** | **Very dark blue-black to black** (looks the same as MDMA) |
| Heroin | Blue-green / teal, often with a dark center |
| Methamphetamine | No reaction, or a pale yellow |
| Amphetamine | No reaction |
| Cocaine | No reaction |
| Ketamine | No reaction |
| LSD | No reaction |
| 2C-I | No reaction |
| Mephedrone (4-MMC) | No reaction |
| PMMA | No reaction |
| Mescaline | Yellow to orange-brown |
| Oxycodone | Ranges from no reaction to light blue-gray or faint pink |
| 5-APB / 6-APB | Black |
| 5-MAPB / 6-MAPB | Black |
| 2C-B | Not enough data to publish a color |
| Sugars / inert fillers | Dark brown to black charring |

Colors follow DanceSafe's Expected Reactions Color Chart, version 17. The headline: **near-black is the "methylenedioxy compound present" signal, blue-green is the "opiate" signal, and no reaction rules out both.**

Two entries deserve a note. Oxycodone used to be listed with a single expected color and now spans a range, which DanceSafe widened after real-world results stopped matching. And 2C-B is deliberately blank rather than guessed at, which is the right call and the standard you should hold any chart to.

## Why the color happens

Mecke is selenous acid dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid. The acid drives a reaction with the target molecule and produces a colored product, and the *structure* of the molecule decides the color. The methylenedioxy ring shared by MDMA, MDA, and the APB compounds produces the intense dark product. The morphinan skeleton in heroin, morphine, and codeine produces the blue-green product. Molecules with nothing for Mecke to grab, like ketamine and cocaine, give nothing.

That is also why Mecke cannot separate molecules that share the key feature. MDMA and MDA both carry the methylenedioxy ring, so **both turn near-black and Mecke cannot distinguish them.** The same logic applies to 5-APB and 5-MAPB.

## How to run the test correctly

1. **Use a tiny sample.** A pinhead-sized grain, roughly 10 mg, on a clean white ceramic or hard plastic surface. More sample does not improve the result, it just floods the color.
2. **Add one drop of Mecke** directly onto the sample. Do not touch the dropper tip to the sample, which contaminates the whole bottle, and do not rest the cap on a dirty surface.
3. **Watch the first minute.** Note the color at 5, 15, 30, and 60 seconds. Speed matters as much as the final hue.
4. **Run a blank.** Put a drop of Mecke by itself next to your test so you can see the reagent's own color for comparison. This is the single easiest way to avoid calling a non-reaction a reaction.
5. **Read under white light** against the printed chart. Colored or dim lighting is the most common cause of a misread.
6. **Use a fresh sample for each reagent.** Do not drip a second reagent onto a spent reaction. Clean up with baking soda, then soap and water.

## Where Mecke actually fits

Mecke is not the first bottle you should reach for. For a sample sold as molly or ecstasy, DanceSafe's current instructions run Marquis first, then Simon's, then Froehde. Mecke earns its place in two situations:

- **Testing an unknown.** When you genuinely do not know what a substance is, Mecke's wide reaction range gives you a fast fork in the road: dark means a methylenedioxy compound, blue-green means an opiate, nothing means neither.
- **Cross-checking an opioid-shaped sample.** Mecke is one of the classic opiate reagents, and blue-green is a distinctive result that few other common substances produce.

What it will not do is confirm anything on its own. Reagents flag red flags, not green lights. Read your results across a single row of the chart: if every reagent you ran matches one substance's row, the result is "expected." If any single reagent falls outside that row, the result is "unexpected" and you should treat the sample as unknown.

## The mistakes that cause misreads

- **Reading too late.** All reagents darken over several minutes no matter what they touched. Judge the first minute.
- **Too much sample.** A heavy pile reads as uniform black and hides the actual hue, which matters enormously when the difference you care about is blue-green versus black.
- **Assuming dark equals MDMA.** Sugars char to dark brown or black too, and so do the APB compounds. Dark is a class signal, not an identity.
- **Skipping the blank.** Old Mecke is dark in the bottle. Without a blank drop for comparison you cannot tell reagent color from reaction color.
- **Treating one reagent as the whole answer.** Mecke confirms a family, not a molecule. Presumptive color tests carry a real rate of ambiguous results.
- **Believing a "right" color means safe.** The color says nothing about dose, nothing about minor adulterants, and nothing about fentanyl.

## What to run alongside Mecke

- **[Marquis](/blog/how-to-read-a-marquis-reagent-result.html)** first. Purple to black means a phenethylamine, orange to brown means an amphetamine-type stimulant.
- **[Simon's](/blog/simons-reagent-color-chart.html)** to separate MDMA from MDA, which Mecke and Marquis both fail to do. Why that distinction matters is covered in [MDA vs MDMA](/blog/mda-vs-mdma.html).
- **A fentanyl test strip** every time. Dissolve the sample in at least 5 mL of water to avoid a false positive, then dip. Full method in our [fentanyl test strip guide](/blog/how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips.html), and strips are available [here](/fentanyl-strips).

## The bottom line

Read Mecke by the color in the first minute under white light with a blank for comparison: **near-black means a methylenedioxy compound like MDMA or MDA, blue-green means an opiate, and no change rules out both families.** Then confirm with Marquis and Simon's, because Mecke cannot tell MDMA from MDA and no reagent can see fentanyl. Reagent tests identify the presence of a substance class. They do not establish purity, they do not establish dose, and they cannot tell you whether something else is also in the bag.
