## Quick answers

**Is Amazon selling fake reagents?** There is no reliable public data on counterfeit rates, and we are not going to invent a number. ## What actually determines whether a reagent works

Most reagents share a design: a reactive component dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid. Marquis is formaldehyde in sulfuric acid, Froehde is molybdic acid, Mandelin is ammonium vanadate. That acid is hygroscopic, so it pulls water out of the air, and a diluted acid drives the color-forming reaction less strongly. Heat and light accelerate the oxidation happening inside the bottle at the same time.

DanceSafe's instructions put a number on the consequence: **most reagents have a shelf life of at least a year when stored in a refrigerator or freezer**, and they should be kept out of heat and sunlight to last as long as possible ([DanceSafe testing kit instructions](https://dancesafe.org/testing-kit-instructions/)).

Read that as a supply chain requirement rather than a storage tip. "At least a year" runs from when the bottle was filled, not from when it reached you, and assumes cool, dark conditions the whole time. A degraded reagent still produces a color, just fainter, slower, or muddier than it should be, and you read it anyway. See our [reagent color chart](/reagent-chart.html) for what a correct reaction looks like.

## The four questions a listing has to answer

Apply these to any seller, on any platform, including the one we link to.

- **Who manufactured it?** Not who is selling it. Who mixed the chemistry. A named manufacturer is accountable for the formulation. An anonymous white-label bottle is not.
- **When was it made or filled?** A date or lot number lets you reason about the shelf life above. No date means an unknown position on the decay curve.
- **How was it stored and shipped?** Refrigerated, warehoused at ambient temperature, or sitting in a delivery van in July? Sellers who care usually say so.
- **Is it the original packaging?** Manufacturer bottles with original labeling, or something decanted into generic droppers by an intermediary?

Marketplace listings frequently answer none of the four. That is not proof of fraud, it is an absence of the information you need to evaluate the product at all.

## Fulfilment and repackaging are the two real unknowns

Third-party fulfilment means inventory can be warehoused for months, and general merchandise warehouses are not temperature-controlled to the standard a corrosive, heat-sensitive acid solution wants. Commingled inventory adds a wrinkle: on some marketplace models, stock from multiple sellers of the same listed item is pooled, so the unit shipped to you is not necessarily the one your chosen seller sent in. None of that is unique to any retailer, and none of it means a given bottle is bad. It means the storage history is opaque to you, and often to the seller.

Repackaging is the other. Reagent components are ordinary laboratory chemicals, and decanting bulk solution into small dropper bottles is a low-barrier business, which produces a spectrum: careful small operations using correct formulations at one end, someone filling bottles with an approximate mixture and printing a label at the other. You cannot tell them apart from a listing photo of a small bottle of clear liquid, and you cannot tell from reviews either. A reagent review is almost always someone reporting that they got a color, and a weak or wrong-colored reaction from a degraded bottle still reads as "it worked" to a buyer with nothing to compare against. Reviews validate delivery, not chemistry.

## What a good listing looks like versus a bad one

**A good listing:**

- **Names the manufacturer**, and that manufacturer has a website you can independently check.
- **States a fill date or lot number**, or at minimum a shelf life policy on the stock it ships.
- **Says something about storage**, such as refrigerated or cool-shipped inventory.
- **Names the reagents included**, not "5-in-1 multi test", and includes instructions with reading windows.

**A bad listing:**

- **Generic branding** on an unnamed bottle, or a brand that exists only on that marketplace.
- **No date, no lot, no shelf life statement** anywhere in the copy.
- **Vague reagent naming**, like "MDMA purity test" with no reagent named. Reagents identify substance classes. None measure purity or dose, and a seller claiming otherwise does not understand the product.
- **Fentanyl strips bundled with no brand named.** Strip sensitivity varies by manufacturer, so the brand is the specification.
- **Outdated color charts.** Ketamine is the tell: **Morris is the current ketamine reagent**, and a listing still advertising Mandelin as the ketamine test is working from stale copy.
