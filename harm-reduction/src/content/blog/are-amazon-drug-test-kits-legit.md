Some are, some are not, and **you usually cannot tell which from the listing page**. Reagent kits are not shelf-stable gadgets. They are small bottles of reactive chemistry whose usefulness depends on who made them, how old they are, and how hot they got on the way to you. Marketplace listings rarely answer any of that, and this applies to eBay, Etsy, a random web store, and Amazon equally.

There is no reliable public data on counterfeit rates, and we are not going to invent a number. The real risk is more mundane than fraud: unknown fill date and uncontrolled storage temperature, neither of which changes how a bottle looks in a photo.

## What actually determines whether a reagent works

Most reagents share a design: a reactive component dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid. Marquis is formaldehyde in sulfuric acid, Froehde is molybdic acid, Mandelin is ammonium vanadate. That acid is hygroscopic, so it pulls water out of the air, and a diluted acid drives the color-forming reaction less strongly. Heat and light accelerate the oxidation happening inside the bottle at the same time.

DanceSafe's instructions put a number on it: **most reagents have a shelf life of at least a year when stored in a refrigerator or freezer**, kept out of heat and sunlight ([DanceSafe testing kit instructions](https://dancesafe.org/testing-kit-instructions/)).

That is a supply chain requirement, not a storage tip. "At least a year" runs from when the bottle was filled, not from when it reached you, and it assumes cool, dark conditions the whole way. A degraded reagent still produces a color, just fainter, slower, or muddier than it should be, and you read it anyway. Our [reagent color chart](/reagent-chart.html) shows what a correct reaction looks like.

## The four questions a listing has to answer

Apply these to any seller, on any platform, including the one we link to.

- **Who manufactured it?** Not who is selling it. Who mixed the chemistry. A named manufacturer is accountable for the formulation. An anonymous white-label bottle is not.
- **When was it made or filled?** A date or lot number lets you reason about the shelf life above. No date means an unknown position on the decay curve.
- **How was it stored and shipped?** Refrigerated, warehoused at ambient temperature, or sitting in a delivery van in July? Sellers who care usually say so.
- **Is it the original packaging?** Manufacturer bottles with original labeling, or something decanted into generic droppers by an intermediary?

Marketplace listings frequently answer none of the four. That is not proof of fraud. It is an absence of the information you would need to evaluate the product at all.

## Fulfilment and repackaging are the two real unknowns

Third-party fulfilment means inventory can be warehoused for months, and general merchandise warehouses are not temperature-controlled to the standard a corrosive, heat-sensitive acid solution wants. Commingled inventory adds a wrinkle: on some marketplace models, stock from multiple sellers of the same item is pooled, so the unit shipped to you is not necessarily the one your chosen seller sent in. None of that means a given bottle is bad. It means the storage history is opaque to you, and often to the seller.

Repackaging is the other. Decanting bulk solution into dropper bottles is a low-barrier business, which produces a spectrum: careful small operations using correct formulations at one end, someone filling bottles with an approximate mixture and printing a label at the other. You cannot tell them apart from a photo of a small bottle of clear liquid, and reviews will not help. A reagent review is almost always someone reporting that they got a color, and a weak or wrong-colored reaction from a degraded bottle still reads as "it worked" to a buyer with nothing to compare against. Reviews validate delivery, not chemistry.

## Good listing, bad listing

A good one names the manufacturer, and that manufacturer has a website you can independently check. It states a fill date or lot number, or at least a shelf life policy on the stock it ships. It says something about storage. And it names the reagents included, not "5-in-1 multi test", with instructions and reading windows.

A bad one carries generic branding on an unnamed bottle, or a brand that exists only on that marketplace, with no date, no lot, and no shelf life statement anywhere. Two other tells:

- **"MDMA purity test" with no reagent named.** Reagents identify substance classes. None measure purity or dose, and a seller claiming otherwise does not understand the product they are selling.
- **Fentanyl strips bundled with no brand named.** Strip sensitivity varies by manufacturer, so the brand is the specification.

And check the color chart for staleness. Ketamine is the tell: **Morris is the current ketamine reagent**, so a listing still advertising Mandelin as the ketamine test is working from old copy.

## The limits worth stating plainly

Even a fresh, correctly stored reagent does not tell you dose or purity. It tells you what class of substance is present, and two reagents plus a fentanyl strip is the practical minimum for a real read. So the Amazon question is really a provenance question, and provenance is answerable. Ask who made it, when it was filled, and how it was stored. A seller who answers all three is worth buying from wherever they are listed. A seller who answers none is selling you an unknown position on a decay curve, and a degraded reagent is worse than no reagent because it still gives you a color to believe.
