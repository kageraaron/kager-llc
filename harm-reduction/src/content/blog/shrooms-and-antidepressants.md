The psilocybin and antidepressant question gets answered backwards almost everywhere online. The fear is serotonin syndrome. The actual finding, in both controlled trials and large survey data, is that **SSRIs and SNRIs mostly blunt psilocybin rather than amplify it**, and that serotonin toxicity from psilocybin plus an SSRI is uncommon because psilocybin does not work the way MDMA works. The genuinely dangerous psychiatric medication in this picture is **lithium**, which is associated with seizures. Here is what the evidence supports, and what it does not.

## Quick answers

**Will antidepressants ruin a mushroom trip?** Often, yes. In a survey of 611 reports of mushroom use during SSRI or SNRI treatment, roughly 47% of SSRI users and 55% of SNRI users described weaker-than-expected effects ([PMID 37291890](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37291890/)). Controlled data is more mixed than that, which is covered below.

**Is psilocybin plus an SSRI dangerous?** The serotonin toxicity risk is lower than commonly believed. Psilocin activates serotonin receptors directly rather than dumping serotonin into the synapse, so it does not stack with an SSRI the way MDMA does ([PMID 34251464](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34251464/)).

**What about lithium?** Lithium is the real hazard. In an analysis of 62 online reports of lithium combined with a classic psychedelic, 47% described a seizure ([PMID 34348413](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34348413/)). If you take lithium, skip psychedelics.

**Do MAOIs blunt psilocybin too?** No. MAOIs go the other direction. Monoamine oxidase breaks down psilocin, so inhibiting it raises exposure and intensity. This is potentiation, not blunting.

**Can I stop my antidepressant so the mushrooms work?** This is the worst option on the list. Stopping abruptly causes discontinuation symptoms, and the blunting persists anyway: about 30% of people still reported reduced effects three to six months after stopping an SSRI or SNRI ([PMID 37291890](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37291890/)).

## How psilocybin works, and why that changes everything

Psilocybin is a prodrug. Your body converts it to psilocin, which acts as a direct agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. It binds the receptor and turns it on.

MDMA does something structurally different. It reverses the serotonin transporter and floods the synapse with serotonin, which then hits every serotonin receptor at once. That flooding is what makes MDMA plus an MAOI potentially lethal, and it is the reason the [MDMA and SSRI interaction](/blog/mdma-ssri-interaction.html) plays out the way it does: the SSRI occupies the transporter MDMA needs, so it partially cancels the drug.

Psilocin does not need the transporter. An SSRI cannot block its access to the receptor. What an SSRI can do is change the receptors themselves. Chronic elevation of synaptic serotonin desensitises and downregulates 5-HT2A receptors over weeks, and that is the leading mechanistic explanation for blunted psychedelic effects on long-term antidepressant treatment. That mechanism is inferred from receptor pharmacology rather than measured directly in people taking mushrooms, so treat it as a plausible explanation rather than a proven one.

## SSRIs and SNRIs: what controlled data says versus survey data

This is where the evidence tiers matter, because the two sources disagree in an interesting way.

**Controlled trial data (higher tier, smaller and shorter).** Becker and colleagues at Basel ran a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study in healthy volunteers, pretreating with escitalopram (10 mg/day for 7 days, then 20 mg/day for 7 days) before a 25 mg dose of psilocybin. Escitalopram had **no relevant effect on the positive mood effects** of psilocybin, but it significantly **reduced bad drug effects, anxiety, and adverse cardiovascular effects** ([PMID 34743319](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34743319/)). A separate open-label study gave 25 mg psilocybin to 19 people with treatment-resistant depression who stayed on their SSRI, and reported no serious treatment-emergent adverse events and no signal of increased suicidal ideation ([PMID 37443386](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37443386/)).

Two weeks of escitalopram is not two years of it, and 19 people is not a safety database. But within those limits, the controlled work does not show the combination being dangerous, and it does not show the trip being erased.

**Survey data (lower tier, much larger and longer exposure).** The Johns Hopkins retrospective survey of 611 reports of mushroom use during SSRI or SNRI treatment found weaker-than-expected effects reported at 47% for SSRIs and 55% for SNRIs, against 29% for bupropion, which is not serotonergic ([PMID 37291890](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37291890/)). Retrospective self-report has obvious problems: no verified doses, no verified substance, recall bias, and people who had an unremarkable experience are less likely to write about it. The bupropion comparison is what makes the pattern hard to wave away.

The honest synthesis: **real-world SSRI and SNRI users on months or years of treatment frequently report blunting, while short controlled pretreatment mainly softens the rough edges.** Duration of treatment is the most likely reason those two findings differ.

The systematic review by Sarparast and colleagues, which examined 24 MDMA and 5 psilocybin interaction studies, reached a similar conclusion about the thinness of the psilocybin evidence base specifically ([PMID 35253070](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35253070/)). Five studies is not a lot to reason from.

## The serotonin toxicity question

Serotonin toxicity needs excess serotonin in the synapse, and it is overwhelmingly a problem of drug combinations that raise intrasynaptic serotonin alongside an MAOI. Malcolm and Thomas reviewed the pharmacology and concluded that **psychedelics without an MAOI present low risk of serotonin toxicity when combined with non-MAOI serotonergic drugs** ([PMID 34251464](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34251464/)).

Psilocin is a direct receptor agonist, not a releaser and not a reuptake inhibitor. It does not raise the serotonin available in the synapse. That is the mechanistic reason the feared scenario is not the likely one.

Low risk is not no risk. Know the signs regardless (Hunter criteria):

- Spontaneous or inducible clonus, which is rhythmic involuntary muscle contraction
- Agitation with sweating
- Hyperreflexia with tremor
- Temperature above 38°C with muscle rigidity

If this appears, stop everything, cool the person, and get to an emergency room. Hyperthermia here comes from muscle activity, so paracetamol and ibuprofen do nothing for it.

## Lithium is the one to take seriously

Nayak and colleagues analysed online experience reports and found that **47% of 62 lithium plus classic psychedelic reports involved a seizure**, plus another 18% describing other medical or psychological emergencies. Zero of 34 lamotrigine reports involved a seizure ([PMID 34348413](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34348413/)). The reports included psilocybin, not just LSD, which is expected given that the whole class shares the 5-HT2A mechanism.

A population-based survey of 613 lifetime psychedelic users found that among those reporting a psychedelic-related seizure, nearly half were on antidepressants, mood stabilisers, or opioid replacement therapy at the time ([PMID 35981469](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35981469/)). A 2024 case report documented new-onset seizures in an adolescent who took LSD on a **low** dose of lithium ([PMID 38986146](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38986146/)), which suggests this is not something you can manage by keeping the lithium level down.

This is case-report-tier evidence and it deserves the usual caveats about unverified doses and unverified substances. It is still the strongest signal in this entire topic, and the direction is unambiguous. Our full write-up is at [LSD and lithium](/blog/lsd-and-lithium.html), and everything in it applies to mushrooms.

## MAOIs, tricyclics, and the rest

**MAOIs potentiate.** Monoamine oxidase A metabolises psilocin, so inhibiting it increases and prolongs exposure. Phenelzine, tranylcypromine, moclobemide, selegiline, and the antibiotic linezolid all count, as do harmala alkaloids in ayahuasca-style preparations. This is the opposite of the SSRI situation: you get more drug, not less, and less predictably. It is also the one class where the serotonin toxicity concern is real.

**Tricyclics appear to amplify.** The available human data is a small retrospective questionnaire study of ten subjects using LSD during antidepressant treatment, which found that chronic tricyclic use was associated with **increased** physical, hallucinatory, and psychological responses, as was chronic lithium, while MAOIs were associated with decreased LSD effects ([PMID 8788508](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8788508/)). Ten people, retrospective, LSD rather than psilocybin, and the MAOI finding conflicts with the pharmacology. Treat the tricyclic signal as a weak reason for caution and dose reduction, not as an established quantity.

**Bupropion and mirtazapine** were not designed around serotonin reuptake and show less blunting in survey data, but neither has controlled interaction data with psilocybin worth relying on.

## The discontinuation trap

Do not stop psychiatric medication in order to trip. Two reasons, and the second one is the one people do not expect.

1. **Discontinuation syndrome is real.** Abruptly stopping an SSRI or SNRI produces brain zaps, dizziness, flu-like symptoms, and mood destabilisation. Stopping lithium abruptly is associated with rebound mania and rapid relapse.

2. **It probably will not even work.** The blunting outlasts the drug. In the Hopkins survey, about **30% of people still reported reduced effects three to six months after stopping** an SSRI or SNRI ([PMID 37291890](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37291890/)). Receptor adaptation does not clear on the same schedule as the molecule.

If you are seriously weighing a taper, that belongs in a conversation with the person who prescribes it.

---

## The bottom line

**On an SSRI or SNRI:** expect a weaker trip, especially after long treatment. Serotonin toxicity risk from this combination alone is low, and the controlled data available shows softened negative effects rather than danger. Taking more to compensate is the common failure mode and a bad idea.

**On lithium:** do not. This is the hard stop.

**On an MAOI:** the interaction runs the other way and effects are amplified unpredictably, with genuine serotonin toxicity potential.

**On a tricyclic:** effects may be amplified, on thin evidence. Caution is warranted.
