**K-cramps are not a hangover or a cramp in the ordinary sense. They are a sign that your bladder is actively being damaged.**

Ketamine (K, special K) bladder damage, clinically ketamine-induced cystitis or ketamine-induced uropathy, is a well-documented condition where regular use destroys the lining of the bladder and, in severe cases, the ureters and kidneys. The damage can be permanent. At the worst end, people have needed surgical bladder removal or ended up on dialysis.

## How well documented this is

Shahani first described ketamine-induced cystitis in 2007, in a case series of 9 daily users who presented with sterile bladder inflammation, severe urgency and frequency, and bladder wall thickening on CT. All had stopped responding to standard UTI treatment, because the cause was never bacterial.

A 2008 Hong Kong study expanded that to 59 patients and described the full spectrum, from early cystitis to contracted, scarred bladders and secondary kidney damage. Some of those patients required dialysis.

The largest picture comes from a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 45 studies and 4,921 patients. Urinary frequency appeared in **77.1%**, urgency in **69.9%**, suprapubic pain (the k-cramps) in **60.4%**, and hydronephrosis, meaning kidney swelling from blocked drainage, in **30.2%**. A separate 2022 review found regular ketamine use raises the risk of cystitis symptoms **3 to 4 fold**, and that early cessation is consistently the most effective intervention.

One more thing worth knowing. This was thought to be a recreational-use problem, but a 2024 case report documented ketamine-induced cystitis in a patient receiving therapeutic ketamine for treatment-resistant depression. The risk at therapeutic doses looks much lower. It is not zero.

## Norketamine is what sits against your bladder

Take ketamine by any route and your liver converts it to **norketamine**, the primary metabolite. Norketamine is excreted in urine, so it spends time in concentrated form directly against the bladder lining. The damage is not from the high. It is from what passes through afterward.

A 2015 pathophysiology review identified several overlapping pathways:

- **Direct urothelial toxicity.** Norketamine disrupts the protective glycosaminoglycan layer of the bladder lining, stripping its barrier function.
- **Neurogenic inflammation.** Metabolites activate sensory nerve fibers in the bladder wall, triggering a persistent inflammatory cascade.
- **IgE-mediated inflammation.** An immune response resembling an allergic reaction, with mast cell degranulation in bladder tissue.
- **Chronic fibrosis.** Ongoing inflammation lays down scar tissue, permanently shrinking capacity.

The end result in severe cases is a small, rigid, fibrotic bladder that holds very little urine and hurts nearly all the time. In a series of 14 people whose cystitis was severe enough to require bladder reconstruction surgery, mean bladder capacity before surgery was **50.9 mL**, against a normal 400 to 600 mL. That figure comes from the most severe end of the spectrum, people referred for surgery after at least a year of failed conservative treatment, not from typical users.

## Where you are on the spectrum

This matters, because it determines whether you can still recover.

**Stage 1, early symptoms, reversible.** Increased urinary frequency and urgency, mild burning on urination. These often resolve within weeks to months of stopping.

**Stage 2, active cystitis, potentially reversible.** K-cramps, meaning sharp cramping suprapubic pain. Visible blood in urine. Needing to urinate every 15 to 30 minutes. Recovery is possible but slower, and may need medical support.

**Stage 3, contracted bladder and upper tract involvement, often irreversible.** Severely reduced bladder capacity, ureteral stenosis meaning narrowing of the tubes connecting kidneys to bladder, hydronephrosis, and renal failure in the most severe cases. At this stage surgery may be required, up to and including cystectomy, removal of the bladder.

K-cramps during or after use are a stage 2 signal. If you are there and you keep using, stage 3 is the direction of travel.

## What to do if you have symptoms

1. **Stop or drastically reduce use.** Cessation is the only intervention with consistent evidence behind it. Everything else is damage control. Early cessation reliably improves symptoms; advanced-stage damage does not reliably reverse.
2. **See a urologist, not just a GP.** A general practice visit often ends in antibiotics for a presumed UTI, which will do nothing, because this is not bacterial. You need cystoscopy and possibly imaging. Tell them about the ketamine. They cannot help you without accurate information.
3. **Do not accept "drink more water."** That is UTI advice. This needs specific management: anticholinergics for urgency, NSAIDs for pain, and in some cases intravesical treatments such as sodium hyaluronate or chondroitin sulfate to help restore the urothelial barrier.
4. **Rule out upper tract involvement.** If you have had symptoms for months, ask for a renal ultrasound or CT to check for hydronephrosis. Caught early it can sometimes be managed. Caught late, it may not.

## Who gets it

Daily or near-daily use is the single biggest risk factor across every study. Heavier doses mean more norketamine in the urine, and long duration drives the fibrosis, so cumulative exposure matters as much as any single night. Damage has been documented in daily users consuming as little as 1 to 2 g a day for months. All routes carry risk; intranasal use may concentrate more ketamine in urine than IV, though that is not definitively established. Most cases in the literature involve people who started in their teens or early twenties.

Occasional use, once a month or less, has not been associated with this condition in the literature. That is not the same as an established safe pattern. There is no known threshold, and risk scales with frequency and cumulative dose.

## The honest position

If you use ketamine regularly, k-cramps are not something to push through. They are a measurable sign of ongoing tissue damage. Unlike MDMA neurotoxicity, where the human relevance is still argued over, this one is unambiguous: documented in thousands of patients, confirmed on biopsy, staged radiologically.

The damage is reversible if caught early. The problem is that many people push through the cramps for months before seeking help, and by then some of it is permanent.
