Testing something sold as 2C-B works differently from testing MDMA, and the difference trips people up before they even read the plate. **2C-B does not produce the fast purple-to-black that MDMA gives on Marquis, so a pale, weak, or slow Marquis result on a 2C-B sample is data, not a broken test.** People see that nothing dramatic happened, assume the reagent is dead or their technique was wrong, and discard the most useful thing the test told them: this is not MDMA. The workable protocol is Marquis first as a red-flag screen, Liebermann second because it does more work on the substances actually likely to be in the bag, a fentanyl strip after that, and a milligram scale before you take anything.

## Quick answers

**What color does 2C-B turn on Marquis?** DanceSafe's current guidance gives a wide range: bright yellow through to dark black or green, and every shade in between. What matters more than the shade is what you do not see, because 2C-B does not give the fast purple-to-black that MDMA and MDA give.

**Does a weak Marquis reaction mean the test failed?** No. On a sample sold as 2C-B, a faint or slow reaction is the expected outcome and a fast purple-to-black is the red flag. Run a blank drop of reagent alongside it so you can tell a genuine pale reaction from the bottle's own color.

**Which reagent is most useful for 2C-B?** Liebermann. It reacts fast, it produces a distinctive yellow ring on 2C-B, and it gives separated results for the substances most likely to be there instead: cocaine, MDMA, ketamine, and cathinones.

**Can a reagent confirm my sample is 2C-B?** No. Several substances, including the cathinone dipentylone, produce reactions DanceSafe describes as almost impossible to tell apart from 2C-B. Reagents narrow possibilities and catch substitutions. They do not confirm identity.

**Is pink cocaine the same thing?** Almost never. Powders sold as tusi or pink cocaine usually contain no 2C-B at all. See [2C-B vs tusi](/blog/2cb-vs-tusi-pink-cocaine.html). ## Why a weak Marquis result is the point

The Marquis reaction most people know is MDMA's: a saturated purple darkening to black inside the first minute, driven by the methylenedioxy ring MDMA and MDA share. 2C-B does not have that ring. It is a bromo-dimethoxy phenethylamine, and it reacts differently and far less dramatically.

DanceSafe widened its published 2C-B ranges after real-world results stopped fitting the older narrow entries. The current Marquis range runs from bright yellow to dark black or green, with every shade in between. That is a wide band, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. What the band does not include is MDMA's signature. So the reading that matters is not which yellow you got:

- **Fast purple to black**: not 2C-B. That is an MDMA-class compound.
- **Orange to brown**: an amphetamine-type stimulant, also not 2C-B.
- **Yellow, green, olive, or a slow dark**: inside the expected 2C-B range, and inside several other substances' ranges too. An absence of red flags, not a confirmation.
- **No visible change**: Marquis is not reacting with the dominant compound. Ketamine and cocaine both stay yellow.

DanceSafe states the underlying logic directly: reagents show red flags, not green lights. Our [Marquis color chart](/blog/how-to-read-a-marquis-reagent-result.html) covers the reading window and the common misreads.

**Always run a blank**, one drop of reagent on a clean part of the plate with no sample. Reagents are tinted and darken with age, and the usual reason someone calls a faint 2C-B reaction "nothing" is having no reference for what the untouched reagent looks like today.

## Liebermann is the reagent that earns its place

Liebermann is potassium nitrite in concentrated sulfuric acid. It starts clear, reacts fast, and has to be read inside roughly 40 to 45 seconds before oxidation drifts every result dark. Two things make it the right second reagent here.

**It gives 2C-B a shape, not just a color.** DanceSafe's current entry for 2C-B on Liebermann is light yellow through dark black **with a yellow ring**, and every shade in between. The ring at the droplet's edge is a structural feature rather than one point on a color scale, so it survives the fact that the color range is broad.

**It separates what you are actually likely to have.** Liebermann gives cocaine light yellow through orange to brick red-orange, MDMA and MDA an intense fast brown-black, ketamine a very pale yellow that is easy to mistake for nothing, and cathinones like mephedrone a bright yellow. Marquis stays silent on cocaine and ketamine entirely. Liebermann does not. Full table in our [Liebermann color chart](/blog/liebermann-reagent-color-chart.html).

Because both ranges are wide, other compounds fall inside them. If identity genuinely matters, send a sample to a lab, listed in [where to get drugs tested](/blog/where-to-get-drugs-tested.html).

## The tusi problem breaks single-reagent testing

If you are holding a pink powder sold as tusi or pink cocaine, the odds are strong it contains no 2C-B. Drug checking and forensic datasets repeatedly find ketamine-based mixtures, usually with MDMA. The evidence is in [2C-B vs tusi](/blog/2cb-vs-tusi-pink-cocaine.html); the point here is about method.

**A mixture defeats a single reagent.** Reagent colors are not additive. A compound giving a strong dark reaction visually swamps one giving a pale reaction, so a sample containing MDMA and ketamine reads as MDMA and the ketamine never appears. A single result on a mixed sample is not merely incomplete, it can point you confidently at the wrong conclusion. If a "2C-B" sample gives fast purple-black on Marquis and fast brown-black on Liebermann, treat it as MDMA, with [MDMA's dose and risk profile](/blog/how-to-test-mdma.html).

## The actual protocol

**What you need**: Marquis, Liebermann, a white ceramic [spot plate](/spot-plate) or kit tray, a clean blade, a fentanyl strip, white light, and a milligram scale.

1. **Take about 10 mg**, a match-head grain, crushed fine. More sample makes color harder to read, not easier.
2. **Add one or two drops** without touching the dropper to the powder, which contaminates the bottle.
3. **Run Marquis first**, noting the color at 5, 15, 30, and 60 seconds. Speed is information: a color appearing only after two or three minutes is oxidation, not a result.
4. **Run Liebermann on a fresh sample in a separate well** and read it inside 45 seconds, watching the droplet edge for a ring.
5. **Compare both against blanks under daylight or white LED.** Colored club lighting is the leading cause of misjudged colors, so test at home.

**Then run a fentanyl test strip separately.** No reagent detects fentanyl at the doses that kill people. Dissolve about 10 mg in at least 5 mL of water, dip for 15 seconds, lay flat, read at 2 to 5 minutes. **Two lines is negative, one line is positive, no lines means invalid.** A positive means do not use it. Method in our [fentanyl test strip guide](/blog/how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips.html).

## Weighing matters more here than almost anywhere

2C-B is active in the low tens of milligrams, and a common oral dose sits in the mid-teens to mid-twenties. The whole distance between a manageable experience and an overwhelming one can be under 10 mg of powder, a difference you cannot see. A reagent tells you at best that nothing obviously wrong is present. It says nothing about how much active drug is in front of you. Use a 0.001 g scale for powder, and do not redose during onset, which for oral 2C-B can run 45 to 90 minutes. Redosing into a slow come-up is the most common route to an accidental heavy dose. Ranges and timeline are in our [2C-B guide](/2cb.html).

## The bottom line

On a sample sold as 2C-B, **the absence of a big reaction is the result.** Marquis staying yellow, green, or olive instead of racing to purple-black is the reagent working, and calling that a failed test throws the information away. Run Marquis for the red flag, [Liebermann](/liebermann-kit) for the second data point and the yellow ring, a strip for fentanyl, and a scale before you dose. No color combination confirms identity, none measures dose, and one component of a mixture can hide another.
