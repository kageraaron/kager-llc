You can take far too much psilocybin, but you almost certainly cannot die from psilocybin toxicity itself. There is no established lethal dose in humans, and the estimated ratio between a typical recreational dose and a lethal one is one of the widest of any recreational drug (PMID 15139867). What actually harms people is everything around the molecule: falling, walking into traffic, drowning, acting on a delusion, an unmanaged heart condition, mixing with the wrong medication, and above all eating a mushroom that was never a *Psilocybe* in the first place. That last one is where the real deaths live.

## Quick answers

**Can you fatally overdose on psilocybin?** There is no documented case of a healthy adult dying from psilocybin toxicity alone. Gable's comparative analysis of acute lethal toxicity ranked hallucinogens as having the lowest direct physiological toxicity of the substances reviewed (PMID 15139867).

**How common are mushroom emergencies?** Rare. In a global survey of 9,233 people who had used magic mushrooms in the past year, 19 (0.2%) sought emergency medical treatment, a per-event risk of roughly 0.06%, and all but one were back to normal within 24 hours (PMID 35388724).

**What does taking too much feel like?** Overwhelming intensity rather than physical collapse: loss of the sense of self, inability to track time or place, panic, confusion about what is real, vomiting, and a heart rate that feels alarming. It is psychologically brutal and physiologically survivable.

**What is the actual deadliest risk?** Misidentification. Amatoxin-containing lookalikes such as *Galerina marginata* cause liver failure and are not neutralised by cooking or drying. See our guide on [psilocybin lookalikes and deadly Galerina](/blog/psilocybin-lookalikes-galerina.html).

**Is Amanita muscaria the same thing?** No. The red-and-white fly agaric contains ibotenic acid and muscimol, not psilocybin. It is a different drug with a real toxicity profile including sedation, delirium, seizures, and coma (PMID 30073844).

## Why psilocybin has such a wide margin

Psilocybin is a prodrug. Your body strips a phosphate group off it to make psilocin, which acts mainly at 5-HT2A serotonin receptors in the cortex. Those receptors are not concentrated in the brainstem centres that control breathing and heart rhythm, which is the structural reason psychedelics do not kill the way opioids or depressants do. There is no respiratory depression mechanism to trigger.

The clinical trial record supports this. A meta-analysis of six randomised controlled trials covering 528 participants found the significant acute adverse effects of therapeutic-dose psilocybin were headache, nausea, anxiety, dizziness, and elevated blood pressure, all resolving within 48 hours (PMID 38598236). Those are side effects, not organ damage.

The word "overdose" still means something here. It means a dose that produces an experience you cannot handle, and those are common. Poison centre data from 2000 to 2016 found the most frequent reported effects of psilocybin mushroom exposures were hallucinations, agitation, and tachycardia, with most patients treated and released from the emergency department rather than admitted (PMID 30182795). US poison centre calls involving psilocybin more than tripled among adolescents between 2018 and 2022 (PMID 38416101), which tracks rising availability rather than rising per-dose danger.

## The behavioural risk is the real risk

Researchers running human psychedelic studies are blunt about this. The primary danger identified in formal safety guidelines is overwhelming distress during drug action leading to potentially dangerous behaviour, such as leaving the session environment (PMID 18593734). Physiological safety is not what the safeguards are protecting against.

In practice that means:

- **Falls and heights.** Balconies, stairs, rooftops, cliff edges. Depth perception and motor coordination are both degraded.
- **Traffic.** Do not drive, and do not cross busy roads. Judging speed and distance is unreliable for hours.
- **Water.** Swimming pools, rivers, hot tubs, the ocean. Drowning is a documented psychedelic risk and it does not require a huge dose.
- **Acting on a conviction.** At high doses people can become certain of something that is false, including that they need to escape, that they are already dead, or that a friend is a threat. Certainty plus mobility is the dangerous combination.
- **Heat and dehydration.** Festival settings, extended dancing, forgetting to drink.

Nearly all of this is solved by one intervention: a sober person in a controlled space with the door effectively closed. Our [safe psilocybin trip guide](/blog/safe-psilocybin-trip-guide.html) covers set and setting in detail.

## Where physiological risk is genuine

**Cardiovascular conditions.** Psilocybin reliably raises blood pressure and heart rate (PMID 38598236). In a healthy person that is a non-event. With uncontrolled hypertension, coronary artery disease, a history of arrhythmia, or a structural heart problem, it is a real strain, and the anxiety of a difficult trip compounds it. Clinical trials exclude these people for a reason.

**Serotonin toxicity when combined.** Psilocin alone is not a strong driver of serotonin toxicity. The dangerous pattern is combination, specifically with monoamine oxidase inhibitors. A review of serotonin toxicity in serotonergic psychedelics concluded that psychotropics without MAOI activity are generally low risk in combination with psychedelics, while an MAOI plus a serotonin-increasing drug is the classic high-risk pairing (PMID 34251464). That includes prescribed MAOIs, some antibiotics such as linezolid, and harmala alkaloids in ayahuasca-style preparations. Stacking mushrooms with MDMA, tramadol, or high-dose SSRIs is a separate and less predictable question. Check our [drug interaction guide](/interactions.html) before you combine anything.

**Psychosis risk.** A personal or family history of schizophrenia or bipolar I disorder is a hard exclusion. Prolonged psychotic reactions triggered by hallucinogens are uncommon but real (PMID 18593734).

**Persistent perceptual changes.** Hallucinogen persisting perception disorder, ongoing visual disturbances after the drug has cleared, is described in the literature as uncommon but serious (PMID 35426769). Higher doses and repeated heavy use are the plausible risk factors. Mild trailing for a day or two after a big dose is common and is not HPPD.

## What "took too much" actually looks like, and how to handle it

The experience of a dose that is too high tends to arrive fast, usually within the first 90 minutes, and it feels like losing the thread. Common features: violent nausea or vomiting, a heart rate that feels like a warning, total loss of the boundary between self and room, time behaving strangely enough to be frightening, and the belief that this will never end.

What helps:

1. **Do not add anything.** No redosing, no alcohol, no cannabis. Cannabis in particular can convert a manageable peak into something overwhelming.
2. **Reduce input.** Dim the lights, drop the volume or change the music, get away from crowds and mirrors and screens.
3. **Change posture and location.** Sitting up, moving to a different room, or stepping outside into fresh air with a sitter often resets a spiral.
4. **Slow the breathing.** Long exhale, longer than the inhale. It genuinely lowers the heart rate that is feeding the panic.
5. **Anchor to the clock.** Psilocybin is time-limited and predictable. Most of a mushroom trip is over in four to six hours, which we break down in [how long shrooms last](/blog/how-long-do-shrooms-last.html).
6. **Stay with them.** Presence is the single most effective intervention. Do not leave someone alone to "sleep it off" at a high dose.

Call emergency services for chest pain, seizure, a body temperature that keeps climbing, unresponsiveness, or someone who cannot be prevented from harming themselves. Tell the clinicians what was taken and when. That information changes treatment and it is not the thing they are interested in reporting.

## Amanita muscaria is a different drug

The red-capped fly agaric is sold as a legal "mushroom" product in many places and is frequently confused with psilocybin mushrooms by people buying gummies and capsules. It contains ibotenic acid and muscimol, which act on GABA-A and glutamate systems rather than serotonin receptors. That gives it a genuinely different and less forgiving profile.

Poison centre review of 34 muscimol and ibotenic acid cases found symptoms in nearly all patients within six hours, with gastrointestinal upset, central nervous system depression, and central nervous system excitation. Five patients required intubation, including a three-year-old who spent three days in intensive care. No deaths occurred in that series, and no liver or kidney injury was seen (PMID 30073844). A comparison of *A. muscaria* and *A. pantherina* poisonings found confusion and agitation more common with *muscaria*, and coma more common with *pantherina* (PMID 25173077).

Sedation deep enough to need a breathing tube is not something psilocybin does. If you are taking a product marketed as an Amanita mushroom, you are not taking psilocybin and none of the psilocybin dose guidance applies.

## The bottom line

Psilocybin's direct toxicity is low enough that a fatal pharmacological overdose is not the thing to plan around. Plan around the dose you cannot handle, the environment you are in, the medications you take, and the identity of what you are eating.
