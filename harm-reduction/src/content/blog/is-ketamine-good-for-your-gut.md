**Not in any way that applies to recreational use.** The "ketamine is good for your gut" claim draws on real studies. They're surgery trials where the benefit came from avoiding opioids, a blood marker of inflammation, and mouse experiments. The only human data on recreational use points the other way. Regular, heavy use is linked to stomach inflammation and bile duct damage.

Here are the four lines of research behind the claim, and what each one shows.

## 1. "It gets your bowels working again after surgery"

**What the studies show:** Two Chinese randomised trials gave esketamine (the S-form of ketamine) during gynaecological surgery. In one, with 100 patients, people passed gas for the first time sooner: about 50 hours vs 57 hours. Doctors use that as the sign the bowel has restarted. In the other, with 71 patients, it was 11 vs 14 hours, and post-surgery nausea dropped from 42% to 11%.

**Why it doesn't apply to you:** Opioids are the main reason the bowel stalls after surgery. Ketamine let the anaesthetists use less of them. The second trial said this outright: it was testing an *opioid-sparing* plan. Ketamine wasn't helping the gut. It was replacing something that slows it down. At a party nobody is on an opioid drip, so there's nothing for ketamine to replace.

The broader picture also cuts against it. A 2025 meta-analysis of 55 surgical studies found that ketamine *raised* post-surgery nausea and vomiting by about 46% compared with non-opioid painkillers.

*Evidence tier: randomised controlled trials, but they measured something that doesn't apply to recreational use.*

## 2. "It's anti-inflammatory"

**What the studies show:** A 2012 meta-analysis of six surgical trials (331 patients) found ketamine given during an operation lowered IL-6 afterwards. IL-6 is a blood marker of inflammation.

**Why it doesn't apply to you:** It's one marker in the blood during the hours after major surgery. It wasn't measured in the gut, and the authors noted that no study had shown it changed how patients actually did. Lower inflammation markers after surgery don't mean healthier gut tissue in someone using on weekends.

*Evidence tier: meta-analysis of RCTs, measuring a blood marker only.*

## 3. "It heals colitis"

**What the studies show:** A 2021 study in mice found that a daily dose of (R)-ketamine for a week reduced colitis caused by a chemical called DSS. (S)-ketamine, the other form, didn't help.

**Why it doesn't apply to you:** Ketamine molecules come in two mirror-image forms, R and S. Street ketamine is a 50/50 mix of both. In this study only one form helped, at a daily dose, in mice with colitis caused by a chemical. It's an early lead for developing a new drug. It's not evidence that the ketamine people take recreationally treats colitis, and nobody has tested it in people with inflammatory bowel disease.

*Evidence tier: rodent study.*

## 4. "It fixes your gut microbiome"

**What the studies show:** Several reviews argue that part of ketamine's antidepressant effect might work through gut bacteria, by way of the gut-brain axis.

**Why it doesn't apply to you:** The data underneath those reviews comes from rodents put under stress or bred to model depression. We didn't find a published study that measured gut bacteria in people before and after ketamine. One of the reviews disclosed pharmaceutical industry support. It's a hypothesis, not a finding.

*Evidence tier: rodent studies and narrative reviews.*

## What the human data on recreational use shows

The only human evidence on repeated, recreational-style use comes from heavy users, and all of it shows harm:

- **Stomach inflammation.** 27.5% of 611 people treated for ketamine bladder damage also had upper-gut symptoms, against 5.2% of non-users. Pain just under the ribcage was most common. Some had repeated vomiting or bleeding.
- **Bile duct damage.** About 1 in 10 chronic users had significant liver injury, all caused by damaged bile flow. Many had widened bile ducts that got worse the longer they'd used.
- **Stomach before bladder.** In 84% of the people who had both, stomach symptoms came first, by an average of 4.4 years.

These are heavy, long-term users, mostly in Hong Kong, so they don't describe occasional use. There's still a big difference: the benefits come from single medical doses, mirror-image forms of the molecule, and mice, while the harm is documented in people who use the way people actually use.

## The honest position

There's real research interest in ketamine, and especially (R)-ketamine, for inflammation and gut-brain conditions. Some of it may turn into treatments one day. None of it supports using ketamine for gut health, and the "good for your gut" claim leaves out that ketamine's track record in human guts so far is damage. If you have gut problems and you use ketamine regularly, the ketamine is a more likely cause than a cure.
