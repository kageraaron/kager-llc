A single DanceSafe reagent bottle performs **50 to 75 tests**. Not one, not five. One test is a single drop of reagent on a scraping of material roughly the size of a match head, around 10 mg, which is a fraction of a dose and a tiny fraction of the bottle. That figure is the answer to the objection people actually have, which is that a kit feels expensive for a one-off. It is not a one-off. Spread across 50 to 75 tests, a reagent bottle works out to far less per test than almost anyone assumes before they own one.

## Quick answers

**How many tests are in one reagent bottle?** 50 to 75. That applies to each bottle in a kit, so a kit with several reagents holds several bottles' worth.

**Is a test kit single use?** No. This is the single most common misconception, and it is the reason people hesitate. A reagent bottle is a dropper bottle of liquid that you use one drop at a time.

**How much of my drugs does a test destroy?** About 10 mg, a match-head-sized scraping. You do not test a whole pill or a whole bag.

**Does testing with three reagents use three tests?** It uses one drop from each of three bottles. A Marquis plus Simon's plus Froehde run consumes one test's worth from three separate bottles, not three tests from one.

**Are fentanyl test strips reusable?** No. Strips are genuinely single use, one strip per sample. That is the one supply people routinely under-buy.

## What one test actually consumes

The reason a bottle stretches so far is that a correct test is tiny. *Some links in this post are affiliate links; we may earn a small commission at no extra cost to you.*

1. **Scrape off about 10 mg**, roughly a match head or a grain of rice, onto a clean white non-porous surface. A [ceramic spot plate](/spot-plate) is the purpose-built version, with separate wells so tests do not run into each other.
2. **Add one drop of reagent** from above, without letting the bottle tip touch the sample. Touching the tip to material contaminates the entire bottle and ends its life instantly.
3. **Watch the first 30 to 60 seconds.** That window is where the diagnostic color appears. Compare against a blank drop of the same reagent placed nearby.

That is the whole procedure, and it costs you one drop and a sample you would not have noticed missing. ## Why people think a kit is single use

Almost everyone who has not bought one pictures the wrong object. The mental model comes from pregnancy tests, COVID tests and breathalyzer tubes: a sealed device you use once and throw out. Under that model a reagent kit looks like a per-use purchase, which makes it feel unjustifiable for occasional use, and the decision quietly becomes "not this time."

The physical reality is a small dropper bottle of liquid. It is closer to a bottle of iodine than to a home test device. Nothing about a single test consumes the bottle, and the volume in one bottle is enough for dozens of drops.

Two things reinforce the misconception. Kits ship in a small box that looks like a single-use product, and reagents are usually discussed one test at a time, so nobody mentions the arithmetic. The arithmetic is the whole story: 50 to 75 tests per bottle turns a kit from a per-use charge into a piece of equipment you own.

## What actually limits how many tests you get

The 50 to 75 range is the realistic band, not a guarantee. What moves you toward the low end is almost always technique or storage rather than testing frequency.

- **Wasted drops**: squeezing too hard puts two or three drops where one was needed. One drop is enough for a 10 mg sample. Squeeze gently and stop.
- **Drops that run down the outside**: tipping the bottle too far or squeezing before it is inverted sends reagent down the side of the bottle instead of onto the plate. That is lost volume and a corrosive mess on your fingers.
- **A contaminated spot plate**: reagent residue from a previous test corrupts the next one, which means retesting, which means more drops. Clean and dry the plate between tests, neutralizing with baking soda before you wash it.
- **A contaminated bottle**: touching the dropper tip to material or to a used well ruins the whole bottle at once, whatever its remaining volume.
- **Degradation over time**: this is the quiet one. A bottle stored warm or in sunlight can become unreadable long before the drops run out. See our guide on [whether reagent test kits expire](/blog/do-reagent-test-kits-expire.html) for storage that protects the bottle you paid for.

A [dropper pipette](/pipette) helps with the first two, since it gives finer control over a single drop than squeezing a reagent bottle does.

## Multi-reagent testing draws from several bottles at once

No single reagent identifies a substance on its own, so a proper check uses two or three in parallel. That does not multiply how many tests you have used from any one bottle. It means one drop from each bottle involved.

The [DanceSafe MDMA kit](/mdma-kit) contains Marquis, the two-part Simon's (Simon's A and Simon's B) and Froehde. A full run on a suspected MDMA sample looks like this: three separate scrapings on the plate, one drop of Marquis on the first, Simon's A then Simon's B on the second, one drop of Froehde on the third. You have used one test's worth from three bottles, and each of those bottles still has 50 to 75 tests in it minus the one.

Why bother with all three? Marquis tells you the chemical class, purple to black for MDMA. Simon's is what separates MDMA from MDA, which Marquis cannot do. Froehde acts as confirmation. [Reading a Marquis result](/blog/how-to-read-a-marquis-reagent-result.html) covers the color set in detail, and [which test kit do I need](/which-test-kit.html) lists exactly what ships in each kit.

The practical consequence: if you only ever test one substance, one kit covers you for a long time. Multi-reagent testing consumes bottles in parallel rather than serially, so all your bottles run down at roughly the same rate and reach empty together.

## Fentanyl test strips are the real exception

Everything above applies to reagents. It does not apply to fentanyl test strips, and this is where people under-buy.

**One strip tests one sample, then it is finished.** A strip is a lateral flow immunoassay, the same format as a home COVID test. Once the sample has wicked up the membrane, the strip cannot be reused, re-dipped or dried out and run again. If you are testing four samples across a weekend, you need four strips.

The buying implication matters. The [MDMA kit](/mdma-kit), like the cocaine, LSD and ketamine kits, ships with a fentanyl test strip, but that is a starting supply rather than an ongoing one. Because strips are single-use, a kit's strip covers one sample and no more, so [buy fentanyl strips](/fentanyl-strips) separately if you plan to test repeatedly.

Buy strips by the count you expect to use, not by the box, because they are the part of a testing setup you will run out of first. Our guide to [using fentanyl test strips](/blog/how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips.html) covers the dilution volumes, which differ by substance and matter more than most people realize.

## The takeaway

One reagent bottle is 50 to 75 tests, one test is one drop on a match-head scraping, and multi-reagent runs draw one drop from each bottle rather than several from one. The kit is equipment you own, not a per-use charge, and the honest way to think about value is per test rather than per purchase. The exception is fentanyl test strips, which are single use, are not included with the MDMA kit, and are the supply worth stocking deeper than you think you need.
