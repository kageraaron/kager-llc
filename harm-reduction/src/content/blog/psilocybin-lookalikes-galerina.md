The single most dangerous thing about wild psilocybin mushrooms is not the psilocybin. It is *Galerina marginata*, the deadly Galerina or autumn skullcap, a small brown mushroom that contains amatoxins, grows on the same wood chips as *Psilocybe cyanescens*, and kills by destroying the liver. Amatoxins are heat-stable, so cooking, drying, brewing tea, and lemon-tekking do nothing to them. Poisoning is survivable, but only if treatment starts early, and the symptom timeline is specifically designed to make you think you are fine when you are not.

## Quick answers

**What is the deadliest mushroom mistaken for magic mushrooms?** *Galerina marginata*. It contains alpha-amanitin, the same class of toxin as the death cap, and it fruits on wood chips and rotting conifer wood in the same places and seasons as *Psilocybe cyanescens*.

**Does cooking destroy amatoxins?** No. Amatoxins are heat-stable cyclic peptides. Boiling, sauteing, drying, and acid extraction leave them intact.

**Does blue bruising prove a mushroom is safe?** No. Blue bruising is good evidence that a *specific mushroom* contains psilocybin. It says nothing about the other mushrooms in the same bag, and faint or absent bruising does not rule a *Psilocybe* out.

**How fast do amatoxin symptoms start?** Usually 6 to 10 hours after eating, sometimes up to 24. That delay is the warning sign. Most mushrooms that make you sick quickly are not the ones that kill you.

**What do I do if someone might have eaten a Galerina?** Go to an emergency department now, before symptoms get worse, and bring an uneaten sample of the mushroom. In the US, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 on the way.

## Which species actually get confused

*Psilocybe cyanescens* ("wavy caps") is the flashpoint. In the Pacific Northwest and coastal BC, both it and *Galerina marginata* fruit on landscaping wood chips in autumn, in the same beds, sometimes touching. Both are small, ochre to orange-brown when wet, and fade to tan as they dry. A phylogenetic survey of the genus found that amatoxins in *Galerina* are concentrated in the *G. marginata* species complex, and every sample tested from that complex carried meaningful toxin levels ([PMID 33566818](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33566818/)).

Other pairings worth knowing:

- ***Psilocybe semilanceata*** (liberty caps) grows in grass, not wood. It gets confused with *Pholiotina rugosa* (formerly *Conocybe filaris*), another amatoxin-containing lawn species.
- ***Psilocybe cubensis*** in the wild grows on dung and dung-enriched pasture. Its lookalikes there are mostly *Panaeolus* species, which are not amatoxin producers, though some are still unpleasant.
- **Generic "little brown mushrooms" (LBMs)** are the broader problem. *Galerina*, *Pholiotina*, and *Lepiota* all contain lethal members, and the field marks that separate them from *Psilocybe* are small.

The relevant genera for amatoxin deaths are *Amanita*, *Lepiota*, and *Galerina*, with *G. marginata* the usual fatal *Galerina* ([PMID 29325729](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29325729/)).

## The distinguishing features

| Feature | *Psilocybe cyanescens* | *Galerina marginata* |
|---|---|---|
| **Spore print** | Dark purple-brown to nearly black | **Rust brown** |
| **Ring (annulus)** | Thin cobwebby veil threads only, no persistent ring | **Distinct fibrous ring** on the upper stem |
| **Bruising** | Bruises blue-green readily on stem, cap edge, gills | Never blue. Stem darkens brown to blackish with age |
| **Stem** | Silvery-white fibrils, white flesh | Brownish, often darker toward the base |
| **Gills** | Broadly attached or notched, becoming mottled dark purple-brown | Crowded, attached to slightly decurrent, evenly pale brown to rusty |
| **Habitat** | Wood chips, woody debris, urban landscaping | Decaying conifer wood, stumps, logs, **and wood chips** |

The spore print is the anchor. Purple-brown to black points to *Psilocybe*, rust brown points to *Galerina*. Put a cap gill-side down on foil or half white and half dark paper, cover it with a cup, and leave it several hours. Descriptions here follow the UBC Beaty Biodiversity Museum's *Mushrooms Up!* keys for both species, which flag them as a specific confusion pair on wood chips.

Note the habitat row: it does not separate them. That is the trap. Brown mushrooms on urban wood chips in the Pacific Northwest are at least as often *Galerina* as *Psilocybe*.

## Why the blue-bruising test is not enough

Blue bruising is the result of psilocin oxidizing after tissue damage. It is real, it is useful, and it is not a safety test. Three reasons:

1. **It is a per-mushroom result, not a per-basket result.** If you pick fifty mushrooms and eight of them bruise blue, you have learned about eight mushrooms. A single *Galerina* mixed into that pick is enough to cause liver failure.
2. **Absence of blue does not mean *Galerina*, and it does not mean safe.** Older, dried, sun-bleached, or low-potency specimens can bruise faintly or slowly, and handling in the field smears the result.
3. **It tests for psilocin, not for the absence of amatoxin.** No field test, colour reaction, or reagent kit detects amatoxins in a mushroom. There is no home version of the LC-MS assays used in hospitals.

Blue bruising belongs in your evidence stack alongside spore print, ring, gill attachment, gill colour, and substrate. On its own it decides nothing. A regional [mushroom field guide](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=mushroom+field+guide+regional+identification&tag=ravewellness01-20) matched to where you actually live, plus verification from a local mycological society, is the standard. Photo ID apps are not.

If you are not foraging at all and are working from a known, weighed source, potency variation is the issue instead, and a [milligram scale](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=milligram+scale+0.001g+calibration+weights&tag=ravewellness01-20) plus the dry-weight ranges in our [safe psilocybin trip guide](/blog/safe-psilocybin-trip-guide.html) covers it.

## The amatoxin timeline, and why it kills people

This is the part that matters most, because the poisoning is structured to produce false reassurance.

**Phase 1, roughly 6 to 10 hours after eating (can stretch to 24):** nothing at all, then abrupt severe vomiting, watery diarrhea, and abdominal cramping. People assume food poisoning or a bad batch. Fluid loss can be severe enough to cause dehydration on its own.

**Phase 2, roughly 24 to 72 hours: the false recovery.** GI symptoms settle. The person feels better, sometimes genuinely well, and this is when people leave hospital against advice or decide not to go at all. Underneath, alpha-amanitin is inhibiting RNA polymerase II in hepatocytes, protein synthesis is failing, and liver enzymes are climbing. The classic description of the syndrome is an asymptomatic latency, then severe GI illness, then acute liver failure ([PMID 29627659](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29627659/)). Amatoxins also recirculate through the bile back into the liver, so damage continues while the person feels fine.

**Phase 3, roughly 48 to 96 hours:** jaundice, coagulopathy, encephalopathy, acute liver failure, sometimes kidney injury. Around half of patients who progress to full liver failure need a transplant ([PMID 29325729](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29325729/)).

**The single most useful rule: onset later than 6 hours is the red flag.** Most gastrointestinal-irritant mushrooms make you sick within 30 minutes to 3 hours and you recover. Cyclopeptide mushrooms are quiet for 6 or more hours and then present. Cyclopeptide-containing species account for more than 90% of fatal mushroom poisonings ([PMID 39882097](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39882097/)). If a mushroom meal produces GI symptoms after a long silent gap, treat it as amatoxin exposure until a hospital says otherwise.

## Treatment: hospital only, and early

There is no home management. Care is aggressive IV fluid resuscitation to maintain urine output, activated charcoal, and antidotal therapy. Intravenous silibinin (Legalon SIL), a milk thistle flavonolignan that blocks hepatic reuptake of amatoxin and interrupts its enterohepatic recirculation, is the most-used antidote. A registry of close to 1,500 cases reported mortality under 10% with IV silibinin compared with over 20% for other regimens ([PMID 22352731](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22352731/)). Note that this is registry and case-series evidence, not a randomized trial, and no antidote has RCT support.

N-acetylcysteine, high-dose penicillin G, extracorporeal removal techniques, and liver transplantation are all used. Current reviews emphasize that outcome depends on toxin identification and how early risk is assessed and treatment begins ([PMID 42188618](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42188618/)). Oral milk thistle capsules from a health store are not a substitute for IV silibinin.

## What to do if someone may have eaten one

1. **Go to an emergency department immediately.** Do not wait for symptoms, and do not wait for symptoms to get worse. The window where treatment works best is before liver injury is measurable.
2. **Call Poison Control on the way.** US: 1-800-222-1222. They coordinate with the hospital and can reach mycologists.
3. **Bring a physical sample.** An uneaten mushroom, a piece from the bag, the substrate it grew on, or even vomit in a bag. Species ID changes management. Photos of the intact mushroom, including the stem base and gills, help.
4. **Say exactly what was eaten and when.** The clock matters more than almost anything else. Give the ingestion time, not the symptom time.
5. **Do not let anyone go home during phase 2.** Feeling better between 24 and 72 hours is the expected course of a serious poisoning, not evidence of recovery. Serial liver function tests and INR are what settle it.
6. **Anyone who shared the meal needs assessment too**, including people with no symptoms.

## The bottom line

Psilocybin itself has a wide safety margin, which we cover in [can you overdose on magic mushrooms](/blog/can-you-overdose-on-mushrooms.html). The lethal risk in wild mushroom use lives almost entirely in identification, and it is a genuine one: a heat-stable liver toxin, a fooling recovery phase, and no field test that can rule it out. Spore prints and expert verification are the standard, and if you cannot get to that standard, do not eat wild mushrooms.
