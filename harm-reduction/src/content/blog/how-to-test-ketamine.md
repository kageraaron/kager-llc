Ketamine is tested with **Morris reagent**, which is unusual because it comes in two parts: Morris A and Morris B, applied to the same sample one after the other and then stirred. DanceSafe reports ketamine finishing **purple** with Morris, and describes it as the only drug they know of that ends purple, while most non-reacting substances end up a dull green. Older guidance online still says to use Mandelin, and that guidance is out of date: **DanceSafe no longer recommends Mandelin for ketamine because the reaction is inconsistent and produces false positives.** A Morris test tells you whether you likely have ketamine. It does not tell you dose, purity, or, in every case, whether you have a substituted dissociative sold under the ketamine name.

## Quick answers

**Which reagent tests ketamine?** Morris. It is a two-part reagent: Morris A (a pink liquid) and Morris B (a clear liquid), used together on a single sample.

**Should I use Mandelin?** No. DanceSafe states it no longer recommends Mandelin for ketamine due to inconsistency and false positives. A lot of older forum guidance has not caught up.

**What colour is ketamine with Morris?** DanceSafe's chart shows ketamine finishing purple. Check the colour chart that ships with your kit against current manufacturer guidance, since reactions and charts get revised.

**Can Morris detect 2-FDCK or DCK?** Partly. DanceSafe reports those two analogs turning a dark blue or grey rather than purple, which is a meaningful difference. Do not assume every dissociative analog separates this cleanly.

**Does the kit test for fentanyl?** The kit ships with one fentanyl test strip, and strips are single-use, so one strip covers one sample.

## Why Morris replaced Mandelin

For years the standard advice for ketamine was Mandelin, and that advice is now stale. DanceSafe's own product material states plainly that they no longer recommend Mandelin reagent for testing ketamine, citing inconsistency and false positives.

The problem with an inconsistent reagent is not that it fails to react. It is that it reacts in a way that overlaps with other substances, so you get a colour, match it to a chart, and walk away with a confident wrong answer. That is worse than no test, because a wrong result changes behaviour in the same direction a right one would. Morris was adopted because it gives a more separable endpoint. If you are working from an old thread or a kit that has sat in a drawer, check which reagent you actually have. Our [Mandelin reagent colour chart](/blog/mandelin-reagent-color-chart.html) covers what Mandelin is still useful for.

## The two-part Morris workflow

This is the part that trips people up. Morris is not one bottle. Both parts go onto the **same** sample, not onto two separate scrapings, and there is a stirring step that most reagents do not have. DanceSafe's instructions for the [ketamine testing kit](/ketamine-kit) run as follows:

1. **Place your sample on a white plate.** Use a white ceramic or hard plastic [spot plate](/spot-plate), not a test tube. Ceramic gives a clean background to read colour against.
2. **Use at least 5 to 10 mg of material**, roughly a match-head scraping. The kit's 10 mg micro scoop takes the guesswork out of this.
3. **Add one drop of Morris A**, the pink liquid, onto the sample.
4. **Add one drop of Morris B**, the clear liquid, onto the *same* sample. Do not start a second scraping.
5. **Stir for a full 30 seconds** with a toothpick or the point of a knife. This step is mandatory. Skipping the stir is the most common way to get a meaningless result from Morris.
6. **Read the final colour against the chart** that came with your kit, in good white light.

Two notes on reading it. **Blue speckles can appear after you add Morris A, and DanceSafe says to ignore them.** They are not the result. And it is the *end* colour that matters, which is a different habit from Marquis, where the fast initial change carries most of the information.

## What the colours mean

These are the reactions DanceSafe describes for Morris on its ketamine kit material:

| Substance | Morris result |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Ketamine** | **Purple / violet** |
| Cocaine | Bright blue |
| DCK and 2-FDCK | Dark blue to grey |
| Most non-reacting substances | Dull green |

DanceSafe's framing is that ketamine is the only substance they know of that finishes purple, which is what makes the test useful. A dull green end colour means you do not have ketamine, and it does not tell you what you do have.

**Check your kit's own chart rather than this table when you run the test.** Formulations and published charts get revised, and the chart in the box is tied to the reagent you actually own. If they disagree, the chart wins. Our [reagent colour chart](/reagent-chart.html) covers the other reagents.

## The substitution problem: 2-FDCK, DCK and MXE

This is the honest limit that matters most for ketamine. **2-FDCK (2-fluorodeschloroketamine), DCK (deschloroketamine) and MXE (methoxetamine) all appear in supply sold under the ketamine name**, sometimes knowingly and sometimes because a seller believes what they were told. They differ from ketamine in potency, duration, and risk profile. DCK and MXE in particular are longer-acting, which is how people end up redosing on a schedule that suits ketamine and getting far deeper than intended.

Morris does some useful work here. DanceSafe reports 2-FDCK and DCK turning a dark blue or grey rather than the purple ketamine gives, so a clearly non-purple result on something sold as ketamine is a signal worth acting on. That is better separation than most reagents manage on closely related compounds.

But be careful how much weight you put on it. **A reagent distinguishes chemical classes, not individual molecules, and dissociative analogs are structurally close enough that clean separation is not guaranteed across the whole family.** We could not verify a specific published Morris colour for MXE from current manufacturer material, so we are not going to invent one. The practical position: a purple result is consistent with ketamine and makes the common analogs less likely, but it is not proof that no substituted dissociative is present. If a batch feels markedly stronger, longer, or different in character from what you know, believe the experience over the colour. See our guide to [ketamine vapes](/blog/ketamine-vapes.html) for how substitution shows up in the vape supply.

The only tools that identify a specific analog with confidence are laboratory methods: mass spectrometry or infrared spectroscopy at a drug checking service, not a colour reaction on a plate.

## Fentanyl strips are a separate test

No reagent detects fentanyl. It is active at doses far too small to move a colour reaction, which is why a strip is a separate step rather than an optional extra. The DanceSafe ketamine kit ships with one fentanyl test strip and a 10 mg micro scoop, and **strips are single-use, so that one strip covers exactly one sample.** If you plan to test more than once, buy [fentanyl test strips](/fentanyl-strips) separately and in quantity.

**How to run it:**

1. Dissolve about 10 mg of your sample in at least a teaspoon (5 mL) of water. More water is safer than less.
2. Dip the strip for about 15 seconds, up to the marked line.
3. Lay it flat and wait 2 to 5 minutes.
4. **Read it: one line is POSITIVE, two lines is NEGATIVE.** No lines means invalid, so use a fresh strip.

That reading direction is backwards from most rapid tests people have used, and reversing it is the single most common mistake in drug checking. One line means fentanyl was detected and you should not use the sample. Full detail in our [fentanyl test strip guide](/blog/how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips.html).

## What a ketamine test cannot tell you

- **Dose**: no reagent measures how much active drug is present. A purple result says nothing about strength, and ketamine potency varies enough between batches that this matters.
- **Purity**: Morris identifies the dominant compound. Cuts and minor adulterants sitting underneath a strong reaction may not show at all.
- **Whether it is a substituted dissociative in every case**: covered above. Morris separates some analogs, not the whole family.
- **Whether it is R or S ketamine**: recreational ketamine is racemic, and no consumer test distinguishes the enantiomers.

And test every batch, not once. A clean result on one bag says nothing about the next one, because supply is not homogeneous. See [do you need to test every batch](/blog/do-you-need-to-test-every-batch.html).

## The bottom line

Use Morris, not Mandelin. Put both parts on one 5 to 10 mg sample, stir for a full 30 seconds, ignore the blue speckles, and read the end colour against your kit's chart. Run a fentanyl strip separately, remembering one line is positive and two lines is negative.
