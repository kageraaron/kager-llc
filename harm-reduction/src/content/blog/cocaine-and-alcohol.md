**Drinking on cocaine is not just two drugs at once. Your liver builds a third one out of them.** When cocaine and ethanol are present together, they react to form cocaethylene, which happens with no other common drug combination. Cocaethylene is more cardiotoxic than cocaine, clears more slowly, and accumulates every time you redose.

Cocaine and alcohol are the most common two-drug pairing in emergency department presentations, and this is why.

## What your liver does with both

Normally an enzyme called carboxylesterase 1, hCE1, breaks cocaine down by cleaving a bond and putting a water molecule in its place. The product is benzoylecgonine, which is pharmacologically inert.

Put ethanol in the liver at the same time and hCE1 uses that instead of water, swapping cocaine's methyl group for an ethyl group off the alcohol. The result, cocaethylene, is structurally almost identical to cocaine with a different tail, and the tail changes how the body handles it.

This reaction, transesterification, only happens when both substances are in the liver together. Harris and colleagues quantified it in a controlled human pharmacokinetic study published in *Drug and Alcohol Dependence* in 2003: roughly **17 percent of cocaine converts to cocaethylene** when alcohol is present. That is not a trace metabolite. It is a sixth of the dose becoming a different drug.

The enzyme is specific. Neither hCE2 nor plasma pseudocholinesterase can catalyse the reaction.

## Why it is worse than cocaine

Cocaethylene shares cocaine's core pharmacology, blocking the dopamine and norepinephrine transporters to produce the same stimulation and euphoria. Four things make it more dangerous.

**It blocks cardiac ion channels harder.** A 2024 systematic review of 42 studies in the *Journal of Clinical Medicine* found cocaethylene is a **threefold more potent blocker of hERG potassium channels** than cocaine, and a more potent sodium channel blocker as well. Both channels are load-bearing for normal cardiac rhythm, and blocking them prolongs the QTc interval, which sets up fatal arrhythmias including torsades de pointes and ventricular fibrillation. Emergency department data match the mechanism: cocaine-alcohol patients show QTc dispersion of 58 to 82 ms against 24 to 43 ms in cocaine-only patients.

**Sudden death risk rises sharply.** Andrews' 1997 postmortem forensic analysis found an **18 to 25 fold increase in sudden death risk** when cocaethylene was present compared with cocaine without alcohol. Evidence tier: case series, not a trial. The signal is consistent across independent datasets.

**Emergency departments see it directly.** Shastry and colleagues ran a prospective cohort at two urban EDs, published in *Academic Emergency Medicine* in 2023, covering 199 patients: 150 cocaine-only and 49 cocaethylene-positive. **Cardiac arrest occurred in 6.1 percent of the cocaethylene group against 0.7 percent of the cocaine-only group** (adjusted OR 12.61, 95% CI 1.10 to 144.18, p=0.048). The confidence interval is enormous, which reflects how few arrests there are in a cohort that size, so treat the point estimate loosely and the direction seriously. Cocaethylene patients also ran higher lactate, a marker of tissue oxygen deprivation, suggesting worse physiological strain even when they did not arrest.

**It hangs around.** Cocaine's elimination half-life is about 1.1 hours; cocaethylene's is about 1.7 in single-dose studies. That gap compounds. Every extra line taken while drinking adds cocaethylene to a pool that is draining more slowly than the cocaine is, so a night of redosing produces more total exposure than the half-life arithmetic suggests.

## Neither drug tells you to stop

The reason this combination is so common is that it feels good and it feels controlled, and both of those are the problem.

Cocaine counteracts alcohol's sedation. It cuts perceived intoxication, restores reaction time and coordination at moderate blood alcohol levels, and removes the drowsiness that would normally register as "that's enough." So people drink substantially more than they otherwise would, not because they wanted to, but because the feedback that usually stops them has been switched off.

It runs the other way too. Alcohol takes the edge off cocaine's anxiety and cardiovascular discomfort, which makes it easier to keep going. Harris' subjects rated the combination as more intoxicating and more pleasurable than either substance alone, which is exactly the mechanism that drives redosing.

A night that starts as a couple of drinks and a couple of lines escalates because neither substance is issuing its usual stop signal. Every round adds to a toxic load that lasts longer than either drug does.

## The liver, over years

Cocaine and alcohol are each hepatotoxic on their own, and together the burden is greater than the sum.

Tamargo and colleagues measured cocaethylene directly in blood by mass spectrometry in 649 participants, publishing in *Drug and Alcohol Dependence* in 2022. People with detectable cocaethylene had **3.17 times the odds of liver fibrosis** compared with non-users (95% CI 1.61 to 6.23, p=0.0008), after adjusting for HIV status, hepatitis C and other covariates. The fibrosis risk from the combination exceeded what cocaine or alcohol contributed individually.

Fibrosis precedes cirrhosis, liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma. This is a risk that accrues over years rather than over a night, which makes it easy to discount and worth knowing about anyway if you combine the two regularly.

## Seizures, in rodents only

Animal data suggest cocaethylene promotes seizures more than cocaine does. One rodent study found 90 percent status epilepticus rates in cocaethylene-treated mice with repeated exposure, higher than with cocaine, and relative toxicity rankings from metabolite work place cocaethylene above cocaine for acute seizure risk.

All of that is rodent data at doses well above human recreational exposure, and no controlled human study has compared seizure thresholds directly. Real enough to be careful about. Not established human pharmacology, and we are not going to present it as though it were.

## Fentanyl is the other problem

Cardiac toxicity is one danger here. Contamination is a separate one that has become just as pressing. Community drug checking services found **fentanyl in roughly 14.8 percent of powder cocaine samples** across 25 US states, and nearly half of US overdose deaths in 2023 involved both opioids and stimulants.

Fentanyl in cocaine is odourless, tasteless and invisible. A test strip is the only way to find it: dissolve a small amount in water and dip. **One line is POSITIVE, fentanyl detected. Two lines is NEGATIVE.** That reading is the reverse of what almost everyone assumes, and it is worth checking twice before you act on it. [DanceSafe sells the strips](/fentanyl-strips) as a nonprofit, and the technique is in the [test kit guide](/test-kits.html).

If a strip comes back positive, use far less than usual, keep naloxone within reach, and do not use alone. A strip tells you a sample contained fentanyl somewhere, not how much or how evenly it was mixed, so a negative result is reassurance rather than a guarantee.

Mixing cocaine and alcohol produces a compound that is more cardiotoxic than either, persists longer, and accumulates as the night goes on, while both drugs quietly suppress the signals that would normally tell you to stop. The threefold ion channel blockade, the cardiac arrest difference in emergency data, and the tripled fibrosis odds all describe the same underlying thing.
