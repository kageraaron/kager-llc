## [1] "What is actually in shroom chocolate bars and gummies?"
- **Queries:** "do mushroom gummies actually have psilocybin", "what's in shroom chocolate bars", "are mushroom gummies real", "polkadot chocolate real psilocybin", "diamond shruumz what happened", "mushroom gummies no psilocybin"
- **What ranks now and why it fails:** Page 1 is almost entirely the vendors themselves — trehouse.com, legalshroom.com, shroomap.com, "Best Shroom Chocolate" listicles — i.e. the exact companies selling the mislabelled product answering the question about whether the product is mislabelled. The good reporting exists (NPR Aug 2024, FDA outbreak pages, a Sept 2025 JAMA Netw Open letter) but is scattered across news sites that don't rank for the buyer-intent phrasing, and none of it is written as a harm reduction answer. Nobody has combined "here is what testing actually found" + "here is how you'd check yours" in one page.
- **Our honest answer:** The strongest single data point is a Sept 2025 JAMA Network Open research letter: 12 retail "magic mushroom" products bought in Portland, Oregon (11 gummies, 1 chocolate) were analysed by GC-MS and LC-MS/MS. **Zero contained psilocybin**, including one labelled "100 mg psilocybin per gummy." Three contained psilocin (pattern consistent with synthetic adulteration, not mushroom material). Two contained undisclosed synthetic tryptamines (mipracetin and 4-hydroxy-diethyltryptamine / 4-HO-DET). One contained kava alkaloids; one contained CBD/THC; one contained caffeine. Four contained no active ingredient at all. Net: 8 of 12 adulterated, 0 of 12 as labelled. Separately, the 2024 Diamond Shruumz outbreak (FDA/CDC): products contained 4-AcO-DMT (psilacetin), psilocin, **pregabalin** in three samples, and ibotenic acid/muscimol in a raw ingredient — with 113+ illnesses, seizures, and deaths under investigation. The honest headline is that "mushroom" on the label predicts nothing about the contents, and the dangerous failure mode is not "it's fake" but "it's a different drug at an unknown dose."
- **Evidence:** **PMID 40932719** — van Breemen RB, Simchuk D, Fritzsche B, Huson D, Kuzdzal SA, Ferguson J. "Active Constituents of Psilocybin Mushroom Edibles." *JAMA Netw Open* 2025 Sep 2;8(9):e2531345. VERIFIED via E-utilities. Diamond Shruumz figures are FDA/CDC outbreak reporting, not a PubMed-indexed trial — cite the FDA outbreak page directly, not a PMID.
- **Links to:** /kits, reagent chart, psilocybin page, `psilocybin-lookalikes-galerina`, `can-you-overdose-on-mushrooms`, planned Amanita post
- **Product tie-in:** Strong and honest — Ehrlich reagent will flag indoles (so a "no colour" result on a supposed psilocybin gummy is informative), and the whole point is that you cannot tell by looking. Note the real limitation: reagents on a chocolate/gummy matrix are messy and won't quantify.
- **Opportunity:** HIGH — already logged in blog-ideas.md as a lead; this verifies the landscape and supplies the anchor citation. The competitive set is literally the vendors, which is the easiest set to outrank on trust signals.

## [3] "Can you overdose just from touching fentanyl?"
- **Queries:** "can you overdose from touching fentanyl", "fentanyl skin contact overdose", "is it safe to touch a baggie with fentanyl", "cop overdosed touching fentanyl", "can you absorb fentanyl through your skin", "do I need gloves to handle fentanyl"
- **What ranks now and why it fails:** This is the clearest case on the site of a **popular answer that is actively wrong**. What ranks is local news coverage of officers "overdosing" from incidental contact, police training material, and rehab sites repeating it uncritically. Medical toxicology has a firm, published, unambiguous rebuttal that almost never surfaces for the consumer phrasing. The few debunks that exist (Slate, Filter, some academic press releases) are written *about police*, not for the person who just found powder in a friend's bag and is scared to touch it.
- **Our honest answer:** No. The American College of Medical Toxicology and American Academy of Clinical Toxicology joint position statement states plainly that **"incidental dermal absorption is unlikely to cause opioid toxicity."** The scale argument is the memorable part: even if you covered **both palms entirely with pharmaceutical fentanyl patches** — an engineered, moist, absorption-optimised matrix, far better at delivery than loose powder — it would take roughly **14 minutes to absorb 100 mcg**. Dry powder resting briefly on intact skin is orders of magnitude worse at crossing the barrier than that. Airborne risk is also low because fentanyl's vapour pressure is extremely low, so it does not meaningfully evaporate; the statement's recommendations are **nitrile gloves for routine handling** and an N95/P100 only in the unusual case of visible airborne powder. The honest secondary point, which is where this becomes harm reduction rather than trivia: the belief causes real harm — bystanders hesitate to start rescue breathing or give naloxone because they are afraid of contamination, and that hesitation is measured in minutes of hypoxia. There is also a legitimate residual caution to state so the post isn't naive: wash hands before eating/smoking, don't touch your eyes or mouth, and never taste-test.
- **Evidence:** **PMID 28842825** — Moss MJ, Warrick BJ, Nelson LS, McKay CA, Dubé PA, Gosselin S, Palmer RB, Stolbach AI. "ACMT and AACT Position Statement: Preventing Occupational Fentanyl and Fentanyl Analog Exposure to Emergency Responders." *J Med Toxicol* 2017 Dec. VERIFIED. Also republished as **PMID 28872357**, same title/authors, *Clin Toxicol (Phila)* 2018 Apr. VERIFIED. Full text open at PMC5711758.
- **Links to:** `how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips`, planned naloxone post, planned Good Samaritan post, cocaine page
- **Product tie-in:** Indirect — naloxone (NEXT Distro, free) and FTS. Don't over-monetise a myth-correction post; the credibility is the point.
- **Opportunity:** HIGH — textbook fit for the site's edge: high emotional search volume, a confidently wrong consensus, a citable toxicology consensus statement, and a direct link to a life-or-death behaviour (whether you help someone).

## [4] "Do fentanyl test strips detect nitazenes?"
- **Queries:** "do fentanyl test strips detect nitazenes", "what are nitazenes", "will narcan work on nitazenes", "does my fentanyl strip detect isotonitazene", "nitazene test strip"
- **What ranks now and why it fails:** Nitazene explainers are almost all UK/EU (they hit the UK supply harder and earlier) or are DEA/public-health bulletins written for clinicians. Nobody answers the actual consumer question — *"I have BTNX fentanyl strips in my bag, do they cover this?"* — and vendors selling FTS have an obvious incentive not to raise it.
- **Our honest answer:** Nitazenes (isotonitazene, protonitazene, metonitazene…) are **benzimidazole** opioids — a completely different chemical scaffold from fentanyl's 4-anilidopiperidine. Fentanyl immunoassay strips are raised against the fentanyl structure, and the published lab evaluation of BTNX Rapid Response FTS (20 ng/mL cutoff, five manufacturing lots) reports cross-reactivity with **fentanyl and eleven fentanyl analogues** — analogues, not benzimidazoles. The clearest practical evidence that FTS do not cover nitazenes is that **BTNX manufactures and sells a separate nitazene test strip**, and the peer-reviewed literature evaluates them as distinct assays. **Honest caveat I must flag: I did not locate a published head-to-head study directly measuring FTS cross-reactivity against nitazene analogues. Say so in the post rather than asserting a clean "no."** Nitazene strips themselves are far from perfect and this is publishable in its own right: the BTNX NTS lab evaluation found isotonitazene LOD of **2000–3000 ng/mL, varying by manufacturing lot**, with 24 of 33 nitazene analogues cross-reacting at ≤9000 ng/mL and **desnitazenes cross-reacting poorly**. A second evaluation found 28 of 36 nitazenes detected (78%), 8 false negatives, and **false positives on seized heroin when caffeine exceeded ~300 µg/mL**. A systematic review adds that heat, >10% acetonitrile, and caffeine adulteration all degrade strip performance. Also worth folding in from the FTS literature: **diphenhydramine, lidocaine, MDMA and methamphetamine cause false positives on fentanyl strips**, and there is notable lot-to-lot sensitivity variation — both facts the kit sellers omit. Naloxone does reverse nitazenes (they are mu-opioid agonists) but higher/repeat dosing is commonly reported.
- **Evidence:** All VERIFIED via E-utilities. **PMID 38238757** Halifax et al., "Testing the test strips: laboratory performance of fentanyl test strips," *Harm Reduct J* 2024 Jan 18 (BTNX FTS lots, fentanyl + 11 analogues, diphenhydramine/lidocaine/MDMA/methamphetamine false positives, lot-to-lot variability). **PMID 39198843** "Nitazene test strips: a laboratory evaluation," *Harm Reduct J* 2024 Aug 28 (isotonitazene LOD 2000–3000 ng/mL, 24/33 analogues, poor desnitazene cross-reactivity). **PMID 40783738** "Evaluation of nitazene immunoassay test strips for rapid in-situ detection of nitazene and nitazene analogs," *Harm Reduct J* 2025 Aug 9 (28/36, 8 false negatives, caffeine false positives >300 µg/mL). **PMID 41531570** Al-Juhani et al., systematic review of nitazene test strip analytical performance, *Cureus* 2025 Dec. **PMID 41312530** Mongold et al., *J Chem Health Saf* 2025 (LOD 0.05 µg/mL; diluents like sugar and flour raise the fentanyl concentration needed for a positive — relevant to real-world sample prep).
- **Links to:** `how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips`, /fentanyl-strips, `how-to-test-cocaine`, planned medetomidine/xylazine posts, planned naloxone post
- **Product tie-in:** Honest and strong — the correct commercial answer really is "FTS do not substitute for nitazene strips," which supports selling/linking both rather than upselling one.
- **Opportunity:** HIGH — timely, no good consumer-facing answer exists, and it is a rare case where the evidence-honest answer ("nobody has published the head-to-head") is itself the differentiator.

## [5] "Does Narcan expire? Can I keep naloxone in my car / festival bag?"
- **Queries:** "does narcan expire", "can you use expired narcan", "expired naloxone still work", "can narcan be left in a hot car", "does narcan go bad in heat", "how to store naloxone", "narcan freezing"
- **What ranks now and why it fails:** This is the single best-evidenced, worst-served question I found. What ranks is Emergent BioSolutions' own product FAQ (legally obliged to say "do not use past expiry"), pharmacy chain blogs repeating the label, and rehab sites. Every one of them gives the liability answer, not the useful one. Meanwhile there are **three separate peer-reviewed studies** that answer it precisely — and the answer is reassuring, which matters because the alternative is someone deciding not to use the kit they have.
- **Our honest answer:** Expired naloxone is almost certainly still good, and heat and cold do not appear to destroy it. Specifically: a stability study of naloxone products beyond their labelled expiration dates found nasal spray retained potency **up to 10 months past expiry** and injectable **up to 19 months past expiry**, at **102.8 ± 2.6%** and **106.0 ± 1.3%** of labelled concentration — both comfortably inside the 90–110% pharmacopoeial window. A separate study exposed naloxone to **80°C heat and −20°C freeze-thaw cycling for up to 28 days** and found **no change in drug concentration**. And because the obvious objection to nasal spray is the device rather than the drug, a third study cycled Narcan nasal devices to **−29°C and 71°C** and found no difference in mean volume dispensed (101.44 µl, 99.01 µl and 108.28 µl vs control) — i.e. the atomiser still fires correctly after a car glovebox in July or a winter night. The correct framing for the post: **the alternative to expired naloxone is no naloxone**, so use what you have and replace it when you can; the studies were done because harm reduction programmes routinely have to decide whether to distribute expired stock. Honest limits to state: these are stability studies, not clinical outcome studies; they tested specific products and durations, so "19 months" is not a licence for five years; and nothing here changes the label or a pharmacist's obligations. Also flag the non-obvious one — heat is not the problem people think it is, but **repeated freeze-thaw of the liquid was also fine**, which is genuinely counterintuitive.
- **Evidence:** All VERIFIED via E-utilities. **PMID 35238012** — "Chemical stability of naloxone products beyond their labeled expiration dates," *J Opioid Manag* 2022 Jan-Feb. **PMID 30813917** — "The effects of heat and freeze-thaw cycling on naloxone stability," *Harm Reduct J* 2019 Feb 27. **PMID 32279692** — "Effect of Extreme Temperature on Naloxone Nasal Spray Dispensing Device Performance," *Prehosp Disaster Med* 2020 Jun.

## [6] "One ear is muffled after the show and it hasn't come back — is that an emergency?"
- **Queries:** "one ear muffled after concert", "hearing loss in one ear after loud music", "ear still blocked 2 days after rave", "muffled hearing won't go away concert", "when to see a doctor after loud music", "sudden hearing loss one ear"
- **What ranks now and why it fails:** The rave/festival hearing content that ranks — including our own three earplug posts — is all **prevention** content. It tells you to wear earplugs. It does not tell the person who is already three days post-show with one dead ear what to do, and that person is searching *right now* with a treatment clock running. What ranks otherwise is generic "tinnitus usually goes away" reassurance from hearing-aid retailers and Healthline, which is the actively dangerous answer here.
- **Our honest answer:** There is a real, time-limited medical emergency hiding inside this query, and the whole rave harm reduction space misses it. Temporary threshold shift (both ears dull, ringing, recovers over hours to a couple of days) is the common and usually benign outcome. But **unilateral** hearing loss that does not recover is a different thing: idiopathic sudden sensorineural hearing loss, and it is treated as an otologic emergency. The AAO-HNS clinical practice guideline (2019 update) says clinicians should obtain audiometry **as soon as possible, within 14 days of symptom onset**, and that **corticosteroids may be offered as initial therapy within 2 weeks of symptom onset**; hyperbaric oxygen is an option within 2 weeks as initial therapy or within 1 month as salvage, and intratympanic steroids are for incomplete recovery at 2–6 weeks. The actionable sentence nobody writes for this audience: *if one ear is clearly worse than the other and has not recovered within about 24–48 hours, that is a same-week ENT or urgent care visit, not a wait-and-see.* Honest caveats to keep the post credible: the guideline addresses **idiopathic** SSNHL, and loud-noise acoustic trauma is not identical to it, so do not overclaim that steroids are proven for the post-rave case; the evidence for steroids in idiopathic SSNHL is itself only "may offer," not "should"; and most muffled ears after a show do recover on their own. The point is that the cost of getting it checked is low and the cost of missing the window is permanent.
- **Evidence:** VERIFIED via E-utilities. **PMID 31369349** — Chandrasekhar SS et al., "Clinical Practice Guideline: Sudden Hearing Loss (Update) Executive Summary," *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2019 Aug. **PMID 31369359** — same authors, full guideline, same journal/date. Supporting: **PMID 35867413** Cochrane review "Intratympanic corticosteroids for sudden sensorineural hearing loss" (2022) — use this to state the evidence is weaker than the guideline's popularity implies. **PMID 21606048** "Sudden sensorineural hearing loss: a review of diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis," *Trends Amplif* 2011.
- **Links to:** hearing page, `can-one-loud-night-damage-hearing`, `do-ears-toughen-up-to-loud-music`, `do-earplugs-ruin-music-at-raves`, `post-festival-recovery`

## [7] "Will these visuals ever go away? Visual snow and trails weeks after tripping (HPPD)"
- **Queries:** "visual snow after shrooms", "still seeing trails weeks after acid", "did I give myself HPPD", "how long does HPPD last", "is HPPD permanent", "HPPD cure", "visual static after psychedelics", "afterimages won't stop"
- **What ranks now and why it fails:** Worst-of-all-worlds competitive set. Rehab and treatment sites dominate and use HPPD as a scare device ("acid causes permanent brain damage"), with no data. Reddit (r/HPPD, r/Psychonaut) has the real accounts plus a lot of catastrophising and folk pharmacology (people recommending each other keppra, clonidine, niacin). Psychedelic-advocacy sites minimise it or skip it. Nobody states the actual evidential position, which is unusually stark and unusually well suited to this site's voice.
- **Our honest answer:** Nobody knows. Concretely: the **entire pharmacological treatment literature for HPPD is 31 studies covering 87 total patients**, of which **28 are case reports or small case series** and only 3 are observational studies reporting substantial symptom reduction. Drugs tried include benzodiazepines (clonazepam), antiepileptics (levetiracetam), antidepressants, alpha-agonists (clonidine) and antipsychotics — with **risperidone notably reported to worsen symptoms**, which is the single most practically important line in the review because antipsychotics are what an uninformed ER or GP is most likely to reach for. Reported outcomes were 28% full recovery and 61% partial recovery within a year, **not statistically significant**. There is no established treatment. There is also no reliable incidence figure — the disorder is defined in DSM-5 but nobody has a denominator, so any site quoting a percentage risk is inventing it. Worth distinguishing in the post: brief, non-distressing visual after-effects (extremely common, usually settle) versus persistent distressing HPPD-2 (rare, poorly understood), and noting the emerging classification work separating flashbacks, HPPD and "reactivations." Practical things that are actually supportable: cannabis and stimulants are the most consistently reported symptom aggravators in the case literature, anxiety amplifies symptom salience, and telling a clinician "risperidone made this worse in the published cases" is a reasonable thing to bring to an appointment.
- **Evidence:** VERIFIED via E-utilities. **PMID 40938689** — "Pharmacological Treatment of Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD): A Systematic Review," *Harv Rev Psychiatry* 2025 Sep 1 (31 studies, 87 patients, 28 case reports/series, risperidone worsening, 28%/61% outcomes, no established treatment). **PMID 41348264** — "Flashbacks, Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD), and Reactivations Following the Use of Classic Psychedelics: Classification and Therapeutic Management," *Curr Top Behav Neurosci* 2026 (current classification framing). **PMID 39741155** — "Neuropsychological profiles of patients suffering from HPPD," *Sci Rep* 2024 Dec 31. **PMID 34456666** — "On Perception and Consciousness in HPPD: A Systematic Review," *Front Neurosci* 2021. **PMID 27822679** — Halpern-lineage review + exploratory study, *Curr Top Behav Neurosci* 2018.

## [8] "My reagent bottle has gone dark / my kit is two years old — is it still good?"
- **Queries:** "do reagent test kits expire", "marquis reagent turned brown", "how long do reagent kits last", "how to store reagent test kit", "do fentanyl test strips expire", "expired fentanyl test strips still work", "reagent kit shelf life", "can I use an old drug test kit"
- **What ranks now and why it fails:** Owned entirely by the kit sellers (DanceSafe, TestKitPlus, Miraculix, Bunk Police), who all give a short self-serving answer — "replace after 12 months," "store in the fridge" — with no data behind it and an obvious incentive to shorten the number. Forums contradict each other. There is no neutral source at all. This is a pure vendor-versus-nobody landscape, exactly the gap the site's positioning is built for.
- **Our honest answer:** Split the question, because the two halves have very different evidence.
  **Immunoassay strips (fentanyl, xylazine, nitazene, benzo):** there is real data and it is reassuring. A 2024 *Forensic Chemistry* evaluation of seven urine immunoassay strip types for drug-checking use found "little to no change in sensitivity was found even though the tests were multiple years expired" for the cocaine, fentanyl and methamphetamine strips. So an expired FTS is very likely still useful, and the same logic as naloxone applies — the alternative is not testing. Two real caveats from the same literature: nearly all strips cross-react with things other than the target analyte, and BTNX fentanyl strips show **notable lot-to-lot sensitivity variation**, so lot matters at least as much as age.
  **Wet chemical reagents (Marquis, Mecke, Mandelin, Simon's, Froehde, Liebermann, Ehrlich):** here the honest answer is **no published shelf-life or stability study exists** — I searched PubMed and found nothing evaluating consumer reagent kit degradation. Say that outright. What can be said mechanistically and is defensible: Marquis is formaldehyde in concentrated sulfuric acid and darkens on air and light exposure; a reagent that has gone amber/brown in the bottle before contacting any sample has oxidised and its colour readings are no longer trustworthy against a chart. The practical protocol — always run a drop on a known-blank surface first to see the bottle's baseline colour, keep bottles cold, dark, tightly capped and upright, and treat any pre-darkened reagent as expired — is sound advice that nobody currently states as *inference rather than fact*. That distinction is the whole differentiator.
- **Evidence:** **PMID 39371371** — "Beyond Fentanyl Test Strips: Investigating Other Urine Drug Test Strips for Drug Checking Applications," *Forensic Chemistry* 2024 Sep. VERIFIED (expired-strip sensitivity finding; universal cross-reactivity finding). **PMID 38238757** — Halifax et al., *Harm Reduct J* 2024 Jan 18. VERIFIED (lot-to-lot variability). **On reagent shelf life specifically: none exists.** No PubMed-indexed study of consumer reagent kit stability was found.
- **Links to:** all six reagent colour-chart posts (`how-to-read-a-marquis-reagent-result`, `ehrlich-`, `froehde-`, `liebermann-`, `mandelin-`, `mecke-`, `simons-`), `how-to-use-fentanyl-test-strips`, /kits, /which-test-kit.html

## [9] "Does a reagent test tell me how much MDMA / how pure it is?"
- **Queries:** "does marquis tell you purity", "how to tell how strong my pill is", "reagent test purity percentage", "does a drug test kit tell you the dose", "my marquis went black instantly does that mean it's strong", "reagent test how much mdma"
- **What ranks now and why it fails:** Vendors bury this (it is the biggest limitation of the product they sell), and Reddit answers it inconsistently — a large fraction of forum answers actively assert that a faster or darker colour change means a stronger/purer sample, which is wrong. The misconception is *dangerous in a specific way*: someone tests a pill, gets a strong black Marquis reaction, concludes "it's good MDMA and it's strong," and doses accordingly — but the reaction says nothing about milligram content, and high-dose pills are the actual mechanism of MDMA harm.
- **Our honest answer:** No. A reagent colour test is a **presumptive identification** test, not a quantification test. It tells you that a compound producing that colour is present.
